# Direction of Intent

## What Jesus said about death, what he did to it, and what is left for those who walk the other way

### Is hell real, and who is going to be there? A study in the series Eyewitnesses of His Majesty, and the Scriptures He Opened to Them

---

## The question, and the picture that gets in its way

Is hell real? And if it is, who is there?

Most people who ask that question already have a picture in their heads, and the picture is the problem. It is a cavern under the earth, red-lit, with fire and pitchforks, nine descending circles, a sign over the gate, and the devil at the bottom running it, into which God throws people for eternity, some of them for a single life of ordinary faults, and most of them for not having heard of him. It is a picture that makes the God who built it a monster and the religion that teaches it a threat, and a great many people have rejected Christianity because of it, reasonably.

Almost none of it is in the Bible. The furniture is Dante's, from a poem written in 1320, with Milton adding to it three centuries later; the single word "hell" that gathers it all together is a translation choice made in 1611 that folded four different words into one; and the God who throws people into it is a figure the Bible's own sentences about the last things do not describe. This study is an attempt to set the picture down and read what the sentences say, in the order the Bible gives them: first what death was, then what Jesus said about it and did to it, then what remains for those who walk away from what he did, then what that state is for, then who the text says is there and who it refuses to name, and last, how a person can know, now, where he is going.

It is written for the reader who wants to be as confident as the evidence allows about what hell is, what Scripture says, and where he stands. The confidence the Bible offers on the last point is greater than most Christians think.

---

## Part One: What death was

Begin where the Bible begins, with the grave.

The Hebrew Bible has one word for where the dead go, and it is not hell. It is *Sheol*, and it occurs sixty-five times, and it means the grave, the pit, the realm of the dead, the place under the earth where everyone goes. Jacob expects to "go down to Sheol" mourning Joseph (Genesis 37:35). The psalmist asks whether God's wonders are known there, "in the land of forgetfulness" (Psalm 88:12). Job calls it "the land of darkness and deep shadow." It is not a place of punishment; it is death's address, and the righteous and the wicked share it. Samuel, called up by the witch of Endor, comes up from it. David expects to go to it and to be with his dead child there. The only hope the Old Testament attaches to Sheol is the hope of being taken out of it: "you will not abandon my soul to Sheol" (Psalm 16:10); "God will ransom my soul from the power of Sheol, for he will receive me" (Psalm 49:15); and, at the very end of the Old Testament's development, the promise that "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:2), the first clear statement of a resurrection with two outcomes.

That is the world Jesus was born into. Death was a descent, for everyone, into a place of shadow, from which God might one day raise his people. The Pharisees believed in that resurrection; the Sadducees denied it; and the ordinary Jew buried his dead facing Jerusalem and waited.

When the Bible was translated into Greek, *Sheol* became *Hades*, the Greek underworld's name, and that is the word the New Testament uses for the same thing: the state of the dead between death and resurrection. Two things should be fixed in mind about it before anything else is said. First, Hades is not hell in the final sense; it is the waiting. Second, Hades ends: in Revelation 20, "Death and Hades" themselves are thrown into the lake of fire, which means that whatever the final state of the lost is, the grave is not it. The grave is a thing that is going to be destroyed.

## Part Two: What Jesus said, and what he did

### What he said

Jesus said less about the state of the dead than people assume, and what he said is consistent.

He called death sleep. "The child is not dead but sleeping," he said of Jairus's daughter, and "our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep," and the church took the word up. He did not mean unconsciousness, because he also said, arguing with the Sadducees about the resurrection, that God "is not God of the dead but of the living," and that therefore Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, dead for two thousand years, are alive to him now (Mark 12:26-27). The dead sleep as to the body and live as to God.

He described the state between death and resurrection exactly once, in the story of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19-31). Both die; both are conscious; the beggar is carried by angels to "Abraham's side" and the rich man is "in Hades, being in torment," with "a great chasm fixed" between. Notice what the torment is. There is a flame, but the rich man's suffering is described as clarity: he can see, across the chasm, the beggar he stepped over every day; he still speaks like a man with servants, asking that Lazarus be sent to cool his tongue and then sent to warn his brothers; and the answer he gets, "they have Moses and the Prophets; if they do not hear them, neither will they be convinced if someone should rise from the dead," is a sentence about the will, not about fire. The rich man is not there because he was rich. He is there because a starving man lay at his gate for years and he never once turned toward him, and now, for the first time, he sees him. Hades in that story is where a life's direction becomes visible to the one who lived it.

He told a dying man, hours before his own death, the plainest thing he ever said about the hour of death: "Truly, I say to you, today you will be with me in Paradise" (Luke 23:43). Today. With me. The thief had asked for nothing but to be remembered, and was promised company.

He said the destination is his presence, not a place: "I go to prepare a place for you... that where I am you may be also" (John 14:2-3). He said the final state is resurrection, of the body, "the resurrection of the just," in which the raised are "like angels," and he staked the claim on himself: "I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live" (John 11:25).

And he said the sentence that already contains the shape of the hell question: "Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul. Rather fear him who can destroy both soul and body in Gehenna" (Matthew 10:28). The death of the body is not the thing to fear. The loss of the whole person is, and that loss is possible, and it is called by the name of a valley.

### What he did

This is the part the furnace picture leaves out, and it is the whole gospel.

He died, and the Bible says where he went. "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit," he said, and then, in the first Christian sermon ever preached, Peter applied Psalm 16 to him: "you will not abandon my soul to Hades, or let your Holy One see corruption... he was not abandoned to Hades, nor did his flesh see corruption" (Acts 2:27-31). Jesus went where the dead go, and was not left there. Paul says he "descended into the lower regions" and then "ascended on high, leading a host of captives" (Ephesians 4:8-9). Peter says he "went and proclaimed to the spirits in prison" (1 Peter 3:19). The creed the church has said for seventeen centuries puts it in four words: "he descended into hell," meaning Hades, the place of the dead. And the risen Jesus, in the first vision of Revelation, describes himself: "I died, and behold I am alive forevermore, and I have the keys of Death and Hades" (1:18).

That is the change. Before the resurrection, every human being went down to Sheol, the righteous to its good side, Abraham's, and waited for a deliverance promised and not yet performed. After it, death has a conqueror who has been through it and holds its keys, and the New Testament's whole vocabulary for a believer's death changes as a result. It is no longer descent. "My desire is to depart and be with Christ, for that is far better" (Philippians 1:23). "We would rather be away from the body and at home with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8). He "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Timothy 1:10). "Through death he destroyed the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil, and delivered all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" (Hebrews 2:14-15). "O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting?" (1 Corinthians 15:55). The waiting room is closed for those who are his; the interim is "with Christ," today, as he told the thief.

And even that is not the end. The Christian hope was never "going to heaven when you die." That is the interim, the resting place; the hope is the resurrection of the body, in a renewed heaven and earth, which is why Paul spends the longest chapter of his longest letter on the resurrection body and not a sentence on harps, and why the Bible's last pages describe not souls ascending but a city coming down. Heaven, in the Bible's sense, is God's presence, and the promise is that his presence will fill a remade earth and that we will walk on it in bodies that do not die.

Hold that in view before turning to hell, because hell is what remains when that is refused.

## Part Three: The words, the poem, and the images

### Four words the King James made one

The English word "hell" is doing the work of four different words, because the translators of 1611 rendered all four with it, and the popular picture is the result. Modern translations distinguish them; the picture has not caught up.

*Sheol* and *Hades* are the grave, the state of the dead, explained above. Neither is the final hell, and both are destroyed at the end.

*Tartarus* occurs once, in 2 Peter 2:4, borrowed from Greek mythology's prison for the Titans, and is used of fallen angels, never of human beings.

*Gehenna* is the word Jesus used, eleven of the twelve times it occurs in the New Testament, and it is a place. *Ge-Hinnom*, the Valley of Hinnom, runs along the south and west of the old city of Jerusalem, and anyone standing on the Temple Mount could point to it. It was where the kings Ahaz and Manasseh burned their own children in the fire to Molech (2 Kings 16:3, 21:6), the worst thing the Old Testament records Israel doing; Jeremiah renamed it "the valley of slaughter" and said it would be filled with the corpses of the judgment to come (7:31-33, 19:6); and the good king Josiah deliberately defiled it, "that no one might burn his son or his daughter as an offering to Molech" (2 Kings 23:10), so that it could never be used for worship again. By the first century it was the byword for God's judgment, and the rabbis had developed a doctrine of Gehinnom as a place of purgation after death, with a famous ruling, preserved in the Talmud, that the ordinary sinner's stay there is twelve months, after which he is either released or ceases. When Jesus said "Gehenna," his hearers heard the valley outside the wall, with its history of burned children and prophesied corpses, and a place of judgment that was not, in their own teachers' view, necessarily endless.

The *lake of fire* is the last term, and it appears only in Revelation 19 to 21. It is the "second death," into which are thrown, in order, the beast and the false prophet, the devil, Death and Hades themselves, and "anyone whose name was not found written in the book of life" (20:15). It is the only place in the Bible where the final destiny of the lost is described as a location, and it is in the most symbolic book of the canon, in a vision that also contains a dragon, a woman clothed with the sun, and a city made of a single pearl per gate.

### The poem

The furniture of the popular hell, the descending circles, the punishments fitted to the sins, the boiling pitch and the frozen lake, the sign "abandon all hope," Satan enthroned at the bottom, is Dante's *Inferno*, written in Italian in 1320, with Milton's *Paradise Lost* (1667) supplying the war in heaven, the fall of the angels, and the devil's magnificent speeches. Dante built his hell out of Aquinas's theology, Virgil's underworld, the medieval imagination, and his own political enemies, several of whom he placed there by name. It is one of the greatest poems ever written and a map of nothing; Dante would have been the first to say it was allegory. The Bible has no circles, no ranked punishments, no devil in charge of hell (in Revelation the devil is hell's prisoner, not its warden), and no Satan with horns, which came from pagan art. When people say they cannot believe in hell, it is very often Dante's they cannot believe in, and they are right not to.

### The images

Strip away the poem and what Scripture says about the final state of the lost is a small set of images, and every one of them was borrowed by Jesus from the Old Testament, where their meaning can be checked.

"Unquenchable fire" and "the worm that does not die" (Mark 9:48) are the last verse of Isaiah, where the redeemed "go out and look on the dead bodies of the men who have rebelled against me; for their worm shall not die, their fire shall not be quenched, and they shall be an abhorrence to all flesh" (66:24). In Isaiah they describe corpses in a valley, the aftermath of judgment, not living people in torment. The fire is unquenchable in the sense that no one puts it out; it burns until there is nothing left.

"Outer darkness" and "weeping and gnashing of teeth" are Jesus's own, and they occur in his parables in one setting only: outside a lit house where a feast is going on. The wedding banquet with the guest who would not put on the garment (Matthew 22); the master returning at night to servants who were not ready (24); the ten bridesmaids, five of whom let their lamps go out and found the door shut (25); the man who buried his talent (25). In every case, the darkness is the dark outside the door of a party, and the people in it are there because they would not come in, or came in on their own terms, or were not watching for the host. The gnashing of teeth, in the Old Testament and in Acts 7:54, is the rage of people who have been shown to be wrong.

"Destruction" and "perish" are the most common words of all. "The gate is wide and the way is easy that leads to destruction" (Matthew 7:13). "Fear him who can destroy both soul and body in Gehenna" (10:28). "Whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life" (John 3:16). "The wages of sin is death" (Romans 6:23). "Eternal destruction, away from the presence of the Lord" (2 Thessalonians 1:9). "The second death" (Revelation 20:14). If one counted the New Testament's vocabulary for the fate of the lost, the words of ending would outnumber the words of ongoing pain many times over.

And the definition, the one place where the state is stated rather than pictured: "They will suffer the punishment of eternal destruction, *away from the presence of the Lord* and from the glory of his might" (2 Thessalonians 1:9). Away from the presence. That is what fire and darkness are both pictures of, which is why the two images can describe one state without contradiction; a fire and a darkness cannot both be literal, as Augustine and Aquinas both observed, and the church's greatest teachers have always said the images are images. What they are images of is absence: the absence of the one in whom "is no darkness at all," outside the city whose "gates will never be shut by day, and there will be no night there."

One more word needs its meaning checked, because the popular picture hangs on it. The word translated "eternal" in "eternal punishment" and "eternal fire" is *aiōnios*, "of the age," from *aiōn*, an age; it is the word Jesus uses for "eternal life," which is not merely endless duration but the life of the age to come. Jude uses it of Sodom, which "serves as an example by undergoing a punishment of eternal fire" (7), and Sodom is not burning now; the fire was eternal in its effect and its source, not in its duration. That does not settle the question of whether the punishment is endless, because *aiōnios* can carry that sense and often does, and because the parallel in Matthew 25:46, "eternal punishment... eternal life," uses the same word for both. It settles only that the word does not, by itself, settle it, which is why the church has room for more than one reading.

## Part Four: Direction of intent

Here is the idea that makes sense of all the images, and it is the Bible's own.

Faith, in Scripture, is not a position but a direction. Before Christians were called Christians, their movement was called "the Way" (Acts 9:2, 19:9, 24:14). The verb the New Testament uses for the believer's life is not "stand" or "hold" but "walk": "walk in the light, as he is in the light"; "walk by faith, not by sight"; "walk in newness of life"; "walk as he walked." The Hebrew word for the whole practice of the Torah, *halakhah*, means "the walking." And the Bible's oldest picture of the moral life is two roads. "See, I have set before you today life and good, death and evil... therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:15, 19). "I set before you the way of life and the way of death" (Jeremiah 21:8). The first psalm opens the whole Psalter with a man on one road and a way that "perishes." Jesus: "the gate is narrow and the way is hard that leads to life, and those who find it are few," beside "the gate is wide and the way is easy that leads to destruction" (Matthew 7:13-14). And the *Didache*, the church's earliest manual, from about AD 100, begins with the sentence: "There are two ways, one of life and one of death, and there is a great difference between the two."

Consider the old proverb that all roads lead to Rome. It was true for one reason: Rome built the roads, from Rome, out to every frontier, so that anyone at the edge of the world could walk to the capital on a road the capital had laid. The Christian claim is that shape exactly: the road was built from the destination outward, by the one who lives there, and it runs to every province. Believing is not standing on the road waiting for Rome to come to you. It is walking it, toward the city with the light on the hill, toward him. And the other choice is not a punishment; it is the other road, the one that leads away from the light into the wilderness on either side, which a traveler can enter by his own choice and leave by his own choice, as the prodigal did, with the father watching the road the whole time. Many roads wind aimlessly and lead nowhere in particular; one path leads to Rome. And the road was there before he lit it. Abel, Enoch, Noah, and Abraham walked toward the city with no map, by the intention of the heart, and some arrived; what Jesus did was not lay a new road but "bring life and immortality to light" (2 Timothy 1:10), which is to say he showed where the old one led, walked it first, and gave everyone the choice of it. Before that, arriving was possible and not likely. After it, the destination has a name, the road has light on it, and the walk is a choice made in daylight.

On this picture, hell is not a sentence imposed at the end of a good walk for lack of a password. It is where the other road goes, and death is the moment when direction becomes destination. Nobody ends up somewhere he was not heading. This is what Jesus's images say when they are read together: the rich man in Hades sees, at last, the beggar he walked past for years, and his torment is the sight; the guests in the outer darkness are outside a door they would not walk through; the goats are people who never turned toward the hungry and the stranger, and are told, to their astonishment, that they were walking away from Christ every time. C. S. Lewis put the whole doctrine in one sentence: "the doors of hell are locked on the inside." And the Eastern church has held for a thousand years a picture that turns the furnace inside out: there is one fire, the fire of God's love, and it is heaven to those who have turned toward it and torment to those who have turned away; the same light that is joy to the one who wanted it is burning to the one who did not. "God is love," and love, to a will bent on itself, is unbearable.

What God judges, on this reading, is what the Bible always said he judges: the heart. "The LORD looks on the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). "Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also." Daniel "resolved in his heart." Jesus, in the sentence that turns Luke's Gospel toward the cross, "set his face to go to Jerusalem" (9:51). Kierkegaard's definition of a pure heart is "to will one thing." Direction of intent is what the thief on the cross had with one sentence, and what the Pharisees lacked with a lifetime of steps, which is why the parable of the sheep and the goats judges neither creed nor mileage but what people were reaching for when they thought no one was watching.

One guard, so this is not misread. The walking does not earn the city; it is what trust looks like from outside. The thief walked one step, a sentence turned toward the next cross, and was told he would arrive that day. Direction can change in an afternoon. That is the mercy in the picture, and the warning: a man can be facing the wrong way at the end after a life of good steps, and facing the right way after a life of bad ones, and the Bible has examples of both.

## Part Five: What hell is for

Why would a God who is love permit such a state to exist at all?

Because he made creatures who can love, and a creature who can love can refuse to. The alternative to the possibility of hell is not universal bliss; it is a universe of puppets, in which no one can say no to God because no one can say anything. God chose, at Eden, a world with real choosers over a world of things, and the possibility of a final no is the price of the possibility of a real yes. Hell is the doctrine that choices are real and that the last one is final. Remove it, and either God overrides the will of the creature who has said no, which is the coercion the objector otherwise accuses him of, or nothing anyone does makes any final difference, and the architects of the Holocaust and its victims end alike. The seriousness of freedom requires that its refusal be possible.

And the doctrine is taught most insistently by the one who died to keep anyone from it. Nearly every hard sentence about Gehenna in the New Testament is in Jesus's own mouth, and he speaks of it the way a man speaks of a cliff he is pulling people back from, and then goes over it himself. "Fear him who can destroy both soul and body in Gehenna" is said by the one who would shortly be destroyed, body and soul, in the valley of the cross, outside the same wall, to keep the people he was warning from that end. The cross is God's own answer to hell: not a decree abolishing it from above, but a descent into it from inside, so that the one who holds the keys is the one who has been there.

Three things follow that the popular picture gets backward. Hell was not made for people: the eternal fire is "prepared for the devil and his angels" (Matthew 25:41), and Revelation's lake receives the devil, the beast, the false prophet, death, and Hades before it receives any human name; the people who go there go where they were not meant to go. The devil is not in charge of it: in Revelation he is its prisoner, not its warden, and the medieval picture of demons with pitchforks running a torture chamber is Dante, not John. And God does not want anyone there: "he is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Peter 3:9); "who desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4); "as I live, declares the Lord GOD, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live; turn back, turn back from your evil ways, for why will you die?" (Ezekiel 33:11). The last sentence is God asking the question the objector asks, and answering it: why will you? It is a question about direction.

## Part Six: What Christians actually hold, and where they differ

Honesty requires saying that serious Christians have held more than one view of the final state of the lost, from the beginning, and that the Bible's images, read without Dante, leave room for the disagreement.

**Eternal conscious torment.** The lost exist forever in a state of punishment, aware and unending. This is the majority view of the Western church since Augustine, held by Aquinas, Calvin, and most of the confessions, and it rests on Matthew 25:46 ("eternal punishment" parallel to "eternal life"), Revelation 20:10 ("tormented day and night forever and ever," said of the devil, the beast, and the false prophet), Revelation 14:11, and the rich man's conscious torment in Hades. Its best modern defenders (Lewis among them) hold it in the form Part Four describes: not God inflicting pain but the creature's own settled refusal, sustained forever because the refusal never ends.

**Conditional immortality, or annihilation.** The lost are finally destroyed; "the second death" means death; the punishment is eternal in its effect, not in the consciousness of the punished. This view rests on the sheer weight of the Bible's destruction vocabulary listed above, on the Old Testament sources of Jesus's images (Isaiah's corpses, Sodom's eternal fire that is not burning), on the biblical teaching that immortality is a gift given to the saved rather than a property every soul has (1 Timothy 6:16, "who alone has immortality"; Romans 2:7, those who "seek for... immortality"), and on the moral difficulty of endless torment for finite lives. It was held by some in the early church, was the view of John Stott, the most respected evangelical of the twentieth century, and is held by a growing minority of evangelical scholars; the Church of England's doctrine commission in 1995 called it a view a Christian may hold.

**Universal restoration.** All are finally saved; the fire is purifying, and in the end "God will be all in all" (1 Corinthians 15:28). This was held by Origen and by Gregory of Nyssa, one of the architects of the Nicene Creed; its confident form was condemned in 553; and it has been revived in two forms in modern times, the "hopeful" universalism of Hans Urs von Balthasar ("Dare we hope that all men be saved?": we may pray for it, we may not assert it) and the assertive universalism of David Bentley Hart. The church's mainstream has rejected the assertion, because Jesus's own words warn of a real and final loss, while permitting the hope, because Scripture says God desires all to be saved and does not say that his desire will be denied.

**Purgatory**, which should be mentioned because it is constantly confused with hell and is not hell at all: the Catholic teaching that those who die in God's friendship but not yet purified are made ready for his presence, "saved, but only as through fire" (1 Corinthians 3:15), a purification of the saved, not a punishment of the lost. It has the rabbis' twelve months, 2 Maccabees 12's prayers for the dead, and the church's ancient practice of praying for the departed behind it; Protestants reject it as unscriptural; either way it is a doorway into heaven, not a room in hell, and the reader who has heard the two conflated should separate them.

This study does not adjudicate among the first three. It notes that all three take hell seriously, that all three agree a final refusal of God is possible and catastrophic, that all three read the fire and the darkness as images of separation from God rather than as a torture chamber, and that the difference among them is what the images point to at the end: unending refusal, final cessation, or eventual return. Where a reader lands will depend on how he weighs Matthew 25:46 against "destroy both soul and body," and honest Christians have weighed them differently for eighteen centuries. What none of them holds is Dante.

## Part Seven: Who is there

The text is more reticent than the tradition, and the reticence is the point.

The Bible names no human being as being in hell. Not one. Revelation's lake of fire has named occupants, the beast, the false prophet, the devil, Death, and Hades, and then "anyone whose name was not found written in the book of life," which is a category, not a list. Jesus says the eternal fire was "prepared for the devil and his angels," and does not attach a human name to it. The church, in two thousand years, has canonized thousands of people and has never once declared, by name, that any person is damned; not Judas, not Nero, not Hitler. It has said it does not know, because the judgment of persons belongs to God, who alone sees the direction of a heart at the last, and Paul's rule stands: "Who are you to pass judgment on the servant of another? It is before his own master that he stands or falls" (Romans 14:4). The Christian who announces which of his neighbors is in hell has taken a seat that was not offered to him.

But the text does say whom Jesus warned, and it is not whom the popular picture expects. He almost never threatens Gehenna to the people the religious called sinners. The tax collectors, the prostitutes, the Samaritans, the Roman soldiers, the woman caught in adultery, the thief on the cross: to these he says "your sins are forgiven," "neither do I condemn you," "today you will be with me." The people he warns about hell are, overwhelmingly, the religious insiders: the Pharisees, "you serpents, you brood of vipers, how are you to escape being sentenced to Gehenna?" (Matthew 23:33), said to the most devout men in Israel; the disciples themselves, about the eye and the hand that cause them to stumble, and about causing "one of these little ones" to sin (Mark 9:42-48); and the people who will say "Lord, Lord" and be told "I never knew you" (Matthew 7:21-23). The rich man in Hades was a respectable man. The goats in Matthew 25 are astonished to be goats. Everyone Jesus warns about hell is someone who was sure he was not going there.

Two of his parables should be read together, because together they refute the password theory of salvation from both sides. In Matthew 25 the sheep are welcomed with "I was hungry and you gave me food," and they do not know what he means: "Lord, when did we see you hungry?" They served him without knowing it was him; they were walking toward him in the dark. In Matthew 7 the people who prophesied and cast out demons in his name are told "I never knew you; depart from me, you workers of lawlessness." They said the name constantly and were walking the other way. What mattered in both was not the word but the direction, which he could see and they could not. The people welcomed did not recognize the King; the people refused had been using his name. If that does not unsettle a religious reader, he has not read it.

So: who is there? Those who, shown the light, preferred the dark, and kept preferring it to the end; whose direction of intent, at the last, was away. Whether any particular person is among them, God knows and we do not, and the Bible's own examples warn us not to guess: the thief was saved and the priests were warned, and no one that Friday would have predicted either.

## Part Eight: How you can know where you are going

The Bible does not leave this to feeling, and it does not leave it to the end. It says a person can know, now, and it says so in the plainest sentences it has.

"I write these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God, that you may *know* that you have eternal life" (1 John 5:13). Not hope, not guess: know. "Whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life. He does not come into judgment, but *has passed* from death to life" (John 5:24): has passed, present perfect, a thing already done. "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). "I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand" (John 10:28). "Neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers, nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:38-39). "If you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved" (Romans 10:9).

Notice what those sentences do not say. They do not say "if you have been good enough," because no one has, and the Bible says so of everyone (Romans 3:23). They do not say "if you have done the rituals," because the thief did none. They do not say "if you have understood the doctrine," because the sheep in Matthew 25 had understood nothing. They say: if you have turned toward him and trusted him. Direction of intent. The verb is *pisteuō*, to trust, and trust is a facing, not an achievement.

And the model is the one Jesus himself gave, on the last afternoon of his life, to the last person he spoke to before he died. A criminal, by his own admission justly condemned, with hours to live and nothing to offer, turned his head toward the next cross and said one sentence: "Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom." No baptism. No works. No creed. No time to walk anywhere. One turn of the face, toward the right person. And the answer came before the sun went down: "Today you will be with me in Paradise." Jesus told a parable about exactly this, and it offends people every time it is read. A landowner hires workers at dawn, and again at nine, and at noon, and at three, and at five in the afternoon, an hour before quitting, and at the end of the day he pays them all the same, and the ones who worked twelve hours complain, and he answers, "Am I not allowed to do what I choose with what belongs to me?" (Matthew 20:1-16). The wage is not for distance traveled or hours on the road. It is for being in the vineyard when the day ends. It is not about how long you have walked the road or how many times you wandered off it; it is about intending to be on it, because of where it leads.

That is how you can know. Not by measuring your steps but by noticing which way you are facing, and if you are facing him, you are on the road that leads to the city, and the one who built it has said in as many words that he will not lose you on the way. And if you find you are facing the other way, the road is still there, and it is not too late, and it will not be too late until it is, and no one knows that hour but God. Ezekiel's question is the last word of this study, because it is God's own, asked of everyone who reads it: "Turn back, turn back from your evil ways; for why will you die?"
