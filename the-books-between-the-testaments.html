The Ground

About This Shelf: the Apostles' Wider Desk

Why This Volume Exists

The eight volumes of this series walked the twenty-four books of the Hebrew Bible, the Scriptures Jesus named on the Emmaus road. But the apostles' desks held more than the twenty-four. Jude quoted a book called Enoch as prophecy. Hebrews remembered martyrs whose story is told only in 2 Maccabees. John set a scene at a festival founded in 1 Maccabees. Paul knew the names of Pharaoh's magicians from a tradition Exodus never records. And behind the New Testament's every page stands the literature of the four centuries between Malachi and Matthew: the Greek Bible's extra books, the visionary writings attributed to Enoch and Ezra and Baruch, the library of the Dead Sea community, and the philosophers and historians of Alexandria and Jerusalem. A reader who has walked the whole Bible has earned the right to know what else the Bible's own authors were reading, and this volume walks it: each book described, dated as scholarship dates it, its contents summarized, what it meant to the Jews who wrote and read it, its New Testament echoes listed with the detail the evidence supports, and, for each, the reasons it stayed off the shelf and where it stands today (in the Catholic, Orthodox, and Ethiopian Bibles, in the Protestant Apocrypha, or in no Bible at all).

The Era, and Its Four Libraries

The books gather from roughly 300 BC to AD 100, the centuries in which Israel passed from Persian to Greek to Hasmonean to Roman rule, saw the temple desecrated and rededicated, divided into the parties the Gospels name (Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, Herodians), spread through the Greek-speaking Mediterranean, and waited, in an age its own writers called prophetless, for the consolation of Israel. Four libraries survive from it, and the volume is organized by them. First, the deuterocanonical books, the seven books and several additions that the Greek Bible carried and the Catholic and Orthodox churches receive as Scripture, printed in Protestant Bibles, when printed at all, as the Apocrypha. Second, the pseudepigrapha, writings issued under the names of ancient heroes (Enoch, Moses, the twelve patriarchs, Solomon, Ezra, Baruch, Isaiah), never Scripture in synagogue or church (the Ethiopian church excepted for two of them), but read widely, quoted by the New Testament, and indispensable for understanding its world. Third, the Dead Sea Scrolls' own compositions, the rules, hymns, commentaries, and visions of the Qumran community, unknown until 1947 and, since then, the single most important body of evidence for what Jewish expectation sounded like in the generation before Jesus. Fourth, the Hellenistic Jewish authors, Philo of Alexandria and Flavius Josephus, not Scripture and not claiming to be, but the era's two great witnesses, whose works the church preserved and the synagogue lost.

The Method, and the Conventions

Each entry answers the same questions in the same order: what the book is and how it survives; when and where and in what language it was written; what it contains; what it meant to its readers; Sealed in the New Testament, its echoes and citations, graded honestly (a verbatim quotation, a certain allusion, a probable echo, or a shared tradition are four different things, and the volume says which); and why it is not canonical, with its present standing. The scarlet badge keeps its meaning from the main series, marking an apostolic writer's use of the text in hand, and the reader should notice that the badge here flies over books the apostles read without calling Scripture, which is itself a lesson in how the New Testament's authors read. The blue panel, How the synagogue reads it, appears where the rabbinic tradition's treatment of a book is instructive (Ben Sira quoted in the Talmud, Hanukkah kept without Maccabees, Enoch's cooling reputation). The gold boxes continue.

One Rule for Reading These Books

The volume states its governing principle at the door, because the reader will need it at every entry: quotation is not canonization. When Paul quoted Aratus and Epimenides and Menander to the Athenians and Corinthians, he did not make Greek poets Scripture; he used true words where he found them. When Jude quoted Enoch, he did the same, and the early church, which knew the quotation, still declined the book. The apostles' wider reading shows the world they thought in, the vocabulary they inherited, and the expectations they either confirmed or corrected; it does not extend the shelf. The reverse is also true: a book's absence from the canon does not make it worthless, false, or forbidden, and the synagogue's own term for these writings, "outside books," describes their position rather than their value. The reader walks them here as the apostles read them: with respect, with discernment, and with the Scriptures open beside them.

The Pseudepigrapha

The Enochic Library: the Books of the Watchers and the Son of Man

1 Enoch: What It Is, and How It Survived

1 Enoch is not one book but five, gathered over three centuries under the name of the patriarch who "walked with God, and he was not, for God took him" (Genesis 5:24), the one man in the antediluvian genealogy who did not die, and therefore, in the imagination of the Second Temple period, the one man positioned to have seen heaven and returned with its secrets. The collection survives complete only in Ge'ez, the classical language of Ethiopia, where it is Scripture to this day in the Ethiopian Orthodox Church (the only communion that canonizes it), with substantial portions in Greek and, since 1947, the Aramaic originals of four of its five parts among the Dead Sea Scrolls, eleven manuscripts, some copied around 200 BC, making Enoch one of the best-attested books in the Qumran library. Its five parts, in the order the collection presents them, are: the Book of the Watchers (chapters 1-36), the story of the fallen angels and Enoch's journeys through heaven and earth, composed in the third century BC or earlier and among the oldest Jewish writings outside the Bible; the Book of Parables or Similitudes (37-71), the visions of the Son of Man and the Elect One, the latest section, from the late first century BC or the early first century AD, and the one part not found at Qumran; the Astronomical Book (72-82), a treatise on the sun, moon, and stars and the 364-day solar calendar, whose Aramaic fragments are the oldest of all (third century BC); the Book of Dreams (83-90), including the Animal Apocalypse, Israel's history told as a fable of sheep, bulls, and wild beasts down to the Maccabean revolt (c. 165 BC); and the Epistle of Enoch (91-108), exhortations and woes with the Apocalypse of Weeks, history in ten weeks, from the second century BC.

What It Contains, and What It Meant

The Book of the Watchers expands four verses of Genesis into a cosmology. "The sons of God saw that the daughters of man were attractive" (Genesis 6:1-4) becomes the story of two hundred angels, the Watchers, who under their chiefs Shemihazah and Asael descend on Mount Hermon, swear an oath, take human wives, father the giants who devour the earth, and teach humanity forbidden arts (weapons, cosmetics, sorcery, astrology), so that violence and corruption fill the earth; the archangels intercede; God sends the flood, binds the Watchers "in the valleys of the earth until the day of their judgment," and the spirits of the dead giants become the demons who afflict humanity until the end. Enoch, commissioned to announce the Watchers' doom, journeys through the cosmos, sees the places of punishment and the tree of life, the storehouses of the winds, the fiery abyss where the fallen stars are held, and the chambers where the souls of the dead await judgment, sorted already into the righteous and the wicked. The Similitudes are three "parables" in which Enoch sees the Head of Days (Daniel 7's Ancient of Days) and beside him "that Son of Man," also called the Elect One, the Righteous One, and, twice, the Messiah: named before the creation of the stars (48:2-6), hidden with God from the beginning, revealed to the elect, seated by God "on the throne of his glory" to judge the kings and the mighty, who will beg for mercy and be delivered to the angels of punishment, while the righteous eat with the Son of Man forever (62). The Astronomical Book insists on a 364-day year, twelve months of thirty days plus four intercalary days, a solar calendar in which every festival falls on the same weekday every year, against the lunar calendar Jerusalem kept, a disagreement that helped drive the Qumran community into the desert. The Animal Apocalypse retells history in code (Adam a white bull, the fallen angels stars, Israel sheep, the nations wild beasts, the Maccabean hero a ram with a great horn) and ends with a new house (a new temple) and a white bull (a new Adam) to whom all the beasts are transformed. And the Epistle's Apocalypse of Weeks schedules history to the judgment and the new heaven.

To its Jewish readers the Enochic library answered questions Genesis had left open: where evil came from (not, primarily, from Adam's fall but from the angels' rebellion and its demonic aftermath, an alternative account of the world's corruption that later rabbinic and Christian theology would reject in favor of Genesis 3), what happened to the dead (a differentiated afterlife, with places of rest and torment already assigned before the resurrection), what the calendar should be, how the wicked powers of the present would be judged, and, in the Similitudes, who the coming judge would be. It was, in the two centuries before Jesus, a widely read and deeply influential book, and its vocabulary, the Watchers, the abyss, the chains of darkness, the Son of Man on the throne of glory, the fire prepared for the wicked, was in the air the apostles breathed.

Sealed in the New Testament: Enoch's Echoes

Sealed in the New Testament, and here the grading matters. First, the one verbatim quotation of a non-canonical book anywhere in the New Testament: "It was also about these that Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied, saying, 'Behold, the Lord comes with ten thousands of his holy ones, to execute judgment on all and to convict all the ungodly of all their deeds of ungodliness that they have committed in such an ungodly way, and of all the harsh things that ungodly sinners have spoken against him'" (Jude 14-15). This is 1 Enoch 1:9, the opening oracle of the Book of the Watchers, quoted in close agreement with the Greek and Aramaic texts, introduced as prophecy, and attributed to Enoch with the "seventh from Adam" epithet the book itself uses (1 Enoch 60:8, 93:3). Jude knew the book, held its oracle true, and cited it with the formula he would have used for Isaiah. Second, the certain allusions in the same letter and its twin: "the angels who did not stay within their own position of authority, but left their proper dwelling, he has kept in eternal chains under gloomy darkness until the judgment of the great day" (Jude 6), and "God did not spare angels when they sinned, but cast them into hell (Tartarus) and committed them to chains of gloomy darkness to be kept until the judgment" (2 Peter 2:4), the fallen angels' imprisonment in darkness until the judgment being not a Genesis datum (Genesis 6 records no imprisonment) but the Book of the Watchers' story, and its language (the chains, the darkness, the great day of judgment) the Watchers' language. Third, the probable allusions: 1 Peter's Christ who "went and proclaimed to the spirits in prison, because they formerly did not obey, when God's patience waited in the days of Noah" (3:19-20), the imprisoned spirits of Noah's days being, on the most natural reading, the Watchers of Enoch's book, so that Peter presents the risen Christ doing what Enoch did in the book, announcing to the imprisoned rebels their doom; and the Similitudes' Son of Man "seated on the throne of his glory" (1 Enoch 62:5; 69:27-29), which is the exact phrase of Matthew's Jesus, "when the Son of Man comes in his glory, and all the angels with him, then he will sit on his glorious throne" (Matthew 25:31; 19:28), the judgment scene of the sheep and the goats sharing the Similitudes' furniture (the Son of Man enthroned, the kings and the mighty judged, the righteous vindicated), though whether the Gospel echoes the book or both draw on a shared reading of Daniel 7 is debated, since the Similitudes' date is close to the Gospels' own. Fourth, the shared traditions: the fire of Gehenna, the abyss, the demons as the spirits of the fallen, the angelic hierarchies with their named archangels (Michael and Gabriel appear in Daniel; Raphael and Uriel and the others are Enoch's), all of which the New Testament assumes as furniture without citing a source. The grading, then: one quotation, two certain allusions, several probable, and a shared cosmology.

Why It Is Not on the Shelf

The reasons stack. It is pseudonymous: Enoch did not write it, and its earliest section postdates the last prophets by two centuries. It is composite, five books of different dates under one name. Its cosmology competes with Genesis on the origin of evil, and the rabbis, who located sin in the human heart and its inclination, had no use for a demonology that blamed the angels. Its solar calendar was sectarian, the Qumran community's badge against Jerusalem's. And the rabbinic tradition, which had begun by honoring Enoch, cooled on him markedly: the Targums and some midrashim read Genesis 5:24 as Enoch's death rather than his translation, Genesis Rabbah preserves the view that Enoch was inconsistent, righteous and then not, taken early lest he lapse, a deflation that looks like a reaction against the Enochic literature's exaltation of him. The church's reception followed a curve: the Epistle of Barnabas and Tertullian cited Enoch as Scripture (Tertullian arguing that Jude's citation proved it), Origen was cautious, Athanasius excluded it from his canon list (367), Jerome and Augustine rejected it, and Augustine explained the rejection with the reasoning that has held since: "the writings attributed to Enoch... are rightly judged by prudent men not to be his," their antiquity unprovable and their contents unsound, Jude's citation of one true sentence not certifying the book (City of God 15.23). Enoch survived in the church's memory as the book Jude quoted, in Ethiopia as Scripture, and in the desert caves as the Aramaic manuscripts that, in 1947, put the whole question back on the table.

How the synagogue reads it

the rabbinic Enoch is a smaller figure than the Second Temple Enoch. Where the Enochic literature makes him the seer of all secrets and, in one late text (3 Enoch, a much later mystical work), the transformed angel Metatron, the classical rabbinic sources are reserved: Onkelos renders Genesis 5:24 as a death, some midrashim class Enoch among the "hypocrites" who wavered, and the Watchers' story is generally read back to Genesis 6's plain sense, the "sons of God" being, for many rabbinic interpreters, the sons of the nobles or the judges rather than angels at all, the whole angelic-fall cosmology declined. The synagogue never listed Enoch among the "outside books" by name because it never treated it as a candidate, and the later Jewish mystical tradition's fascination with Enoch-Metatron is a separate stream from the rabbinic mainstream. The divergence is instructive: the New Testament's authors stood closer to the Enochic world (Jude's citation, Peter's imprisoned spirits) than the rabbis did, and both communities, church and synagogue, ended by keeping the book off the shelf.

In plain terms: 1 Enoch is five books over three centuries under the name of the man who did not die: the Watchers' fall (Genesis 6 expanded into the origin of demons), Enoch's cosmic tours, the Similitudes' Son of Man enthroned as judge, the 364-day calendar, and history told as animals and weeks. Complete only in Ethiopic (where it is Scripture), Aramaic at Qumran. Jude quotes 1 Enoch 1:9 verbatim as prophecy; Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4 tell the Watchers' imprisonment in Enoch's words; 1 Peter's "spirits in prison" and Matthew's Son of Man "on the throne of his glory" are probable echoes. Not on the shelf because pseudonymous, post-prophetic, composite, sectarian in calendar, and competing with Genesis on the origin of evil; the rabbis cooled on Enoch, and the church, having cited him early, followed Augustine: Jude's one true sentence does not certify the book.

Jubilees: the Torah Retold in Weeks of Years

Jubilees calls itself "the book of the divisions of the times," and presents itself as revelation dictated to Moses on Sinai by the Angel of the Presence: the story of Genesis 1 through Exodus 12 retold, from creation to the Passover, with the chronology fixed in jubilees (periods of forty-nine years) and weeks of years, so that every event has a date on the 364-day calendar and every festival its patriarchal origin. Written in Hebrew around 160-150 BC, in the generation of the Maccabean crisis, it survives complete only in Ge'ez (the Ethiopian church canonizes it beside Enoch), with Latin fragments and, from Qumran, some fifteen Hebrew manuscripts, more copies than most biblical books, and the Damascus Document cites it by title as an authority. Its retelling has purposes: to show that the law was kept before Sinai (Abraham observing Shavuot and Sukkot, Noah celebrating the feast of weeks, the patriarchs as observant Jews, so that the Torah is as old as creation and its calendar written into the heavens), to defend the solar calendar as the only one on which the festivals can be kept "in their times," to intensify the boundary between Israel and the nations (no intermarriage, no eating with Gentiles, circumcision on the eighth day without exception), to explain evil through the Watchers' fall (borrowed from Enoch) and the demons under their prince Mastema (Jubilees' name for the adversary), and to place the priesthood of Levi and the kingship of Judah at the center of Israel's future. Its readers, in the Maccabean generation and at Qumran, found in it a Torah they could hold against Hellenizing Jews who abandoned circumcision and ate as Greeks: a Torah older than Moses, kept by Abraham, written on heavenly tablets.

Sealed in the New Testament the echoes are of tradition rather than text, and the volume grades them so. The law "delivered by angels" (Acts 7:53, "you who received the law as delivered by angels"; Galatians 3:19, "the law... put in place through angels by an intermediary"; Hebrews 2:2, "the message declared by angels") reflects a widespread Second Temple understanding of Sinai, visible in the Septuagint's Deuteronomy 33:2 ("at his right hand were angels with him") and given its fullest form in Jubilees, where the Angel of the Presence dictates the whole revelation, so that the apostles' phrase is the era's, and Jubilees is its clearest witness. Abraham's trials counted as ten (Jubilees 17:17-18; 19:8) stand behind the later rabbinic "ten trials of Abraham" and the New Testament's treatment of the Aqedah as the test that proved faith (Hebrews 11:17-19; James 2:21-23), Jubilees having first framed Genesis 22 as Mastema's challenge to God about Abraham's loyalty, a Job-shaped wager, and the Aqedah as its refutation. The circumcised heart that God will create in the last days (Jubilees 1:23, "I will circumcise the foreskin of their heart... and I will create in them a holy spirit") is Deuteronomy 30 and Ezekiel 36 fused in a form very like Paul's "circumcision is a matter of the heart, by the Spirit" (Romans 2:29). And the demonology, Mastema, the demons as the giants' spirits, the plagues on Egypt as Mastema's work permitted, belongs to the shared world of the exorcisms in the Gospels. No New Testament author quotes Jubilees; several think in its categories.

Why it is not on the shelf: it is pseudonymous (a Sinai revelation composed in the second century BC), sectarian (its calendar and its rigor mark the Qumran-Essene stream against the Pharisaic-rabbinic mainstream, which kept the lunar calendar and would not have the festivals fixed by a book that called Jerusalem's reckoning corrupt), and secondary by design (a retelling of Scripture, not Scripture). The rabbis never mention it; the church knew it slightly (Epiphanius and others cite it as "the Little Genesis"), never received it, and lost its Greek; and it survived, like Enoch, in Ethiopia and in the caves.

In plain terms: Jubilees (c. 160-150 BC, Hebrew) retells Genesis through Exodus 12 as dictated to Moses by the Angel of the Presence, dating everything in jubilees on the 364-day calendar, showing the patriarchs keeping the festivals before Sinai, drawing hard boundaries against the nations, and placing the adversary Mastema behind evil and the testing of Abraham. Fifteen Qumran copies; Scripture only in Ethiopia. The New Testament thinks in its categories (the law "delivered by angels," the Aqedah as the wager that proved faith, the Spirit-circumcised heart) without quoting it. Off the shelf as pseudonymous, sectarian in calendar, and a retelling rather than a revelation.

The Testament of Moses, and the Body of Moses

Jude's letter carries a second reference to a book no Bible contains: "But when the archangel Michael, contending with the devil, was disputing about the body of Moses, he did not presume to pronounce a blasphemous judgment, but said, 'The Lord rebuke you'" (Jude 9). Deuteronomy records only that Moses died in Moab and "he buried him in the valley... but no one knows the place of his burial to this day" (34:6), and the dispute over the body is nowhere in Scripture. Origen, in the third century, identified Jude's source: "a little book called the Ascension of Moses" (De Principiis 3.2.1), and the surviving work usually called the Testament of Moses (or Assumption of Moses) is the likeliest candidate, though its ending, where the dispute would stand, is lost. What survives is a single Latin manuscript, a sixth-century palimpsest discovered in Milan in 1861, breaking off mid-sentence, a translation of a Greek translation of a Hebrew or Aramaic original. The book is Moses's farewell to Joshua: a prophecy of Israel's history from the conquest through the exile and return to the Hasmonean priest-kings ("they will be called priests of the Most High God; they will work iniquity in the holy of holies"), Herod ("an insolent king... he will judge them as they deserve"), and Herod's sons, which dates the final form to the early first century AD, probably in Jesus's own lifetime or just before. Its climax is a Levite named Taxo, who with his seven sons chooses to die in a cave rather than transgress the law, confident that their innocent deaths will move God to vengeance, followed by a hymn of God's kingdom appearing: "then his kingdom will appear throughout his whole creation, then the devil will have an end, and sorrow will be led away with him," Israel exalted to the stars, the nations judged. Its readers were the pious of the Herodian generation, offered a theology of martyrdom (the Taxo story) and a timetable of deliverance in which the present tyranny was the last before the end.

Sealed in the New Testament Jude 9 is, on Origen's testimony and the book's own shape, a reference to its lost ending, in which Michael disputed with the devil, who claimed Moses's body (in some later versions on the ground that Moses had killed the Egyptian, or that matter belongs to the devil), and Michael, refusing to curse even Satan by his own authority, invoked the Lord's rebuke, the formula of Zechariah 3:2, "The LORD rebuke you, O Satan." Jude's point is the archangel's restraint against the false teachers' recklessness in reviling angelic beings. The reference is certain; the surviving text of the source is not, and the volume says so. Beyond Jude, the Testament's picture of the kingdom's appearing, with the devil's end and the exaltation of Israel, belongs to the same expectation Luke 10:18 ("I saw Satan fall like lightning") and Revelation 20 speak into.

Why it is not on the shelf: pseudonymous, first-century, and lost even to the church that cited it, its whole survival being one broken Latin manuscript. No community ever canonized it.

In plain terms: Jude 9's dispute between Michael and the devil over Moses's body comes from no canonical book; Origen names the source as "the Ascension of Moses," and the surviving Testament of Moses (one broken sixth-century Latin manuscript, composed in Herod's era) is the likeliest candidate, its ending, where the dispute would stand, lost. The book is Moses's farewell prophecy of Israel's history to Herod, a martyr-theology (Taxo and his sons), and a hymn of the kingdom's appearing and the devil's end. Jude used its scene for the archangel's restraint, "The Lord rebuke you," Zechariah 3's formula. Never canonized anywhere.

The Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs: Ethics in the Voice of Jacob's Sons

The Testaments are twelve farewell speeches, one from each son of Jacob on his deathbed, modeled on Genesis 49 and Deuteronomy 33: each patriarch recounts his life (usually a sin, drawn from Genesis or elaborated), draws its moral (Reuben on lust, Simeon on envy, Judah on greed and drunkenness, Gad on hatred, Joseph on chastity and forgiveness), and prophesies his descendants' future, with a recurring instruction to honor Levi and Judah, from whom the priesthood and the kingship, and in some passages a priestly and a royal Messiah, will come. The work survives in Greek (with Armenian and Slavonic versions), and its origins are the most debated of any book in this volume: a Jewish core, probably composed in Greek in the second century BC (Aramaic and Hebrew Levi and Naphtali documents at Qumran and in the Cairo Genizah show the Testament of Levi has an older Semitic source), was transmitted by Christians and contains passages that are unmistakably Christian (references to God appearing in the flesh, to the crucifixion, to the Gentiles' salvation through Jesus), so that scholars divide between a Jewish work with Christian interpolations and a Christian composition using Jewish sources. The volume takes the majority view, a Jewish ethical work of the second century BC retouched by Christian hands, and reads its ethics as evidence of the moral world Jesus's hearers lived in. That world is remarkable. The Testaments summarize the law as the love of God and the love of neighbor, together, a generation before the Gospels ("Love the Lord and your neighbor; have compassion on the poor and weak," Testament of Issachar 5:2; "Love the Lord through all your life, and one another with a true heart," Testament of Dan 5:3). They teach forgiveness of a repentant offender and the danger of harbored anger in terms that sound like the Sermon on the Mount ("If anyone sins against you, speak peaceably to him... and if he repents and confesses, forgive him... and if he is shameless and persists in his wrongdoing, even so forgive him from the heart," Testament of Gad 6:3-7). They hold up Joseph as the model of the righteous sufferer who was slandered, enslaved, imprisoned, and forgave, "whom the Lord exalted." And they anticipate a priestly Messiah from Levi in language of startling elevation (Testament of Levi 18: "the Lord will raise up a new priest... his star shall rise in heaven as a king... the heavens shall be opened, and from the temple of glory sanctification shall come upon him... and the spirit of understanding and sanctification shall rest upon him... and he shall open the gates of paradise, and shall remove the threatening sword against Adam") and a royal Messiah from Judah (Testament of Judah 24: "a star shall arise to you from Jacob in peace, and a man shall arise from my seed like the sun of righteousness... and the heavens shall be opened to him to pour out the Spirit"), which, on the interpolation theory, are partly Christian, and, on any theory, show what messianic expectation could sound like in the period.

Sealed in the New Testament the double love-commandment as the law's summary (Mark 12:29-31 and parallels) has its nearest pre-Christian parallels here, and the Testaments show that Jesus's pairing of Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18 stood within a Jewish reading already available, his originality lying in the authority with which he made the pairing the whole law's hinge; the forgiveness teaching of Testament of Gad 6 parallels Luke 17:3-4 ("if your brother sins, rebuke him, and if he repents, forgive him") closely enough that both may draw on a common ethical tradition; Paul's "the wrath has come upon them at last" (1 Thessalonians 2:16) has a near-twin in Testament of Levi 6:11; the opened heavens and the descending Spirit at the priest-king's coming (Testament of Levi 18; Judah 24) are the furniture of the Gospels' baptism scene, though the interpolation question makes the direction of influence uncertain; and Joseph as the forgiving righteous sufferer is a type the Gospels' passion narratives and Stephen's speech in Acts 7 assume. Grading: no quotation, one close verbal parallel (Thessalonians), several shared ethical traditions, and a messianic vocabulary of contested origin.

Why it is not on the shelf: pseudonymous (twelve dying patriarchs speaking Greek ethics), of mixed and uncertain authorship, and preserved only by the church, which read it for edification (it circulated in Greek and Latin through the Middle Ages, and was translated into English by the sixteenth century) and never canonized it. The synagogue did not know it in this form.

In plain terms: Twelve deathbed speeches in the voices of Jacob's sons (second-century BC Jewish core, Greek, retouched by Christian hands): each confesses a sin, draws a moral, and prophesies, honoring Levi and Judah from whom priest and king will come. Its ethics are the moral world of Jesus's hearers: the law summarized as love of God and neighbor together, forgiveness of the repentant "from the heart," Joseph as the forgiving righteous sufferer, and messianic passages of the opened heavens and the descending Spirit whose origin is contested. Close parallels to the double commandment, Luke 17's forgiveness rule, and 1 Thessalonians 2:16, no quotations. Off the shelf as pseudonymous and of mixed authorship; read by the church, unknown to the synagogue in this form.

The Deuterocanon

The Maccabean Books: the Revolt, the Martyrs, and the Festival Jesus Kept

1 Maccabees: the Chronicle of the Revolt

1 Maccabees is a history, and the best one the period produced: the story of the Jewish revolt against the Seleucid empire from the accession of Antiochus IV Epiphanes (175 BC) to the death of Simon, the last of the five Maccabee brothers (134 BC), written in Hebrew (the original is lost; the Greek translation survives, and Jerome saw the Hebrew) by a Judean author around 100 BC, in the style of the books of Samuel and Kings, with dated events, quoted documents, and a sober prose that rarely mentions God's name directly ("Heaven" is the book's usual reverent circumlocution). Its narrative runs: Antiochus's Hellenizing program and the Jerusalem elite's collaboration (a gymnasium built, circumcision abandoned, "they joined with the Gentiles and sold themselves to do evil"); the king's plunder of the temple and his decree abolishing the Torah, the Sabbath, and the sacrifices on pain of death; the "abomination of desolation" set up on the altar in December 167 BC (the phrase the book uses, 1:54, is Daniel's); the spark at Modein, where the priest Mattathias kills a Jew about to sacrifice on a pagan altar and the royal officer with him, and flees to the hills with his five sons; the guerrilla war of Judas, called Maccabeus ("the Hammer"), his victories at Emmaus and Beth-zur, and the rededication of the cleansed temple on the twenty-fifth of Kislev, 164 BC, exactly three years after its desecration, "and Judas and his brothers and all the assembly of Israel determined that every year at that season the days of dedication of the altar should be observed with joy and gladness for eight days" (4:59), the founding of Hanukkah; Judas's death in battle, the leadership of Jonathan and then Simon, the treaties with Rome and Sparta, the expulsion of the Seleucid garrison from the citadel, and the Jewish assembly's decree making Simon "leader and high priest forever, until a trustworthy prophet should arise" (14:41), the Hasmonean dynasty founded. To its readers, the Hasmonean court's own generation, the book was national epic and dynastic charter: the family that saved the Torah, the temple, and the nation deserving the crown and the high priesthood it had taken, with the Hellenizers as the traitors and the martyrs (the mothers who circumcised their sons and were thrown from the walls with the infants at their necks; the thousand who refused to fight on the Sabbath and were slaughtered in their caves) as the nation's seed.

Sealed in the New Testament the Gospel of John records that "at that time the Feast of Dedication took place at Jerusalem. It was winter, and Jesus was walking in the temple, in the colonnade of Solomon" (John 10:22-23), the feast being Hanukkah, founded in 1 Maccabees 4 and mentioned in no book of the Hebrew Bible, so that the fourth Gospel sets one of its great discourses (the sheep who hear his voice, "I and the Father are one") at a festival whose only Scriptural charter is this book, Jesus keeping a Maccabean holiday in the colonnade where the Maccabees' successors had walked. Second, the book's twice-repeated confession of the prophetless age, "there was great distress in Israel, such as had not been since the time that prophets ceased to appear among them" (9:27), and the stones of the defiled altar stored "until a prophet should come to tell what to do with them" (4:46), and Simon's rule "until a trustworthy prophet should arise" (14:41), is the era's own testimony to the silence this series called Amos's famine, the four centuries the Baptist's voice ended, and the New Testament's "a prophet has arisen among us" (Luke 7:16) lands on ears trained by exactly this waiting. Third, the "abomination of desolation" (1:54) shows how Daniel's phrase was read in the second century BC, as Antiochus's altar, and therefore what Jesus's hearers understood when he reused it of a desolation yet to come (Matthew 24:15). No quotation; two certain historical connections (Hanukkah, the prophetless age) and one interpretive one.

2 Maccabees: the Martyrs, the Resurrection, and the Prayer for the Dead

2 Maccabees is not a sequel but a parallel account, covering a shorter span (from the high priest Onias III, c. 180 BC, to Judas's victory over Nicanor in 161 BC), and a different kind of book: the author tells us it is an abridgment of a five-volume history by Jason of Cyrene (2:23), written in Greek, in the rhetorical style Greek historians called "pathetic," designed to move, with miracles, prayers, angelic horsemen, and speeches, composed probably around 124 BC and prefaced by two letters from Jerusalem urging the Jews of Egypt to keep Hanukkah. Its theology is explicit where 1 Maccabees is reticent: God's providence is named, the temple is the book's beating heart, the nation's sufferings are discipline rather than abandonment ("these punishments were designed not to destroy but to discipline our people," 6:12), and the martyrs are the book's glory. Chapters 6 and 7 tell what 1 Maccabees only alludes to: the aged scribe Eleazar, ninety years old, refusing to eat pork or even to pretend to, choosing the rack "so that the young may learn to die willingly and nobly for the sacred and holy laws"; and the mother with her seven sons, tortured to death one by one before her eyes, each brother making his confession, the second declaring, "the King of the universe will raise us up to an everlasting renewal of life," the third offering his tongue and hands, "from heaven I got these, and for his laws I disdain them, and from him I hope to get them back again," the fourth, "it is better to die at the hands of men and to cherish the hope God gives of being raised again by him; but for you there will be no resurrection to life," and the mother, encouraging each son in "the language of their ancestors," reasoning from creation to resurrection: "the Creator of the world, who shaped the beginning of humankind and devised the origin of all things, will in his mercy give life and breath back to you again, since you now forget yourselves for the sake of his laws" (7:23). The book's other famous passage is Judas's sacrifice for his fallen soldiers, found wearing pagan amulets: he takes up a collection and sends it to Jerusalem "to provide for a sin offering... taking account of the resurrection. For if he were not expecting that those who had fallen would rise again, it would have been superfluous and foolish to pray for the dead... Therefore he made atonement for the dead, so that they might be delivered from their sin" (12:43-45), the one text in any Bible that prays for the dead and calls the practice holy.

Sealed in the New Testament the writer to the Hebrews, in the roll of faith, passes from named heroes to unnamed ones and writes: "Women received back their dead by resurrection. Some were tortured, refusing to accept release, so that they might rise again to a better life. Others suffered mocking and flogging, and even chains and imprisonment" (Hebrews 11:35-36). "Tortured, refusing to accept release, so that they might rise again to a better life" is the seven brothers and Eleazar to the letter: the torture (the Greek word is the rack's), the offered release (Antiochus's repeated offers of clemency for a single bite), the refusal, and the motive, resurrection to "a better life" (the brothers' "everlasting renewal of life"), so that the New Testament's great chapter on faith counts among the faithful the martyrs of a book outside the Hebrew canon, without naming it, as it names none of its exemplars after the judges. The reference is as certain as an unquoted allusion can be, and the reader should notice its weight: Hebrews places the Maccabean martyrs in the same roll as Abraham and Moses. Beyond it, 2 Maccabees' theology of resurrection is the clearest pre-Christian statement of bodily resurrection outside Daniel 12, and the Sadducees' denial of it in the Gospels is the denial of exactly this Pharisaic-Maccabean hope; its picture of martyrdom as expiation ("I, like my brothers, give up body and life for the laws of our ancestors, appealing to God... and through me and my brothers to bring to an end the wrath of the Almighty," 7:37-38) is a bridge between Isaiah 53 and the New Testament's atonement vocabulary; and its account of the martyrs' mother reasoning from creation to resurrection is the argument Paul makes from the God "who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (Romans 4:17).

Why the Maccabean Books Are Not on the Hebrew Shelf, and Where They Stand

The reasons are the era's: both books narrate events of the second century BC, well after the prophetic succession the synagogue held had closed with Malachi, and both say so themselves, the prophetless age being their own repeated lament; 1 Maccabees was Hebrew and Judean but a dynastic chronicle, and the Hasmonean dynasty it glorified ended, in the rabbis' memory, in the Sadducean high priests who persecuted the Pharisees, so that the book had no constituency in the academies that fixed the canon; 2 Maccabees was Greek and Alexandrian, a Greek abridgment of a Greek history for the diaspora. The Hebrew original of 1 Maccabees was lost; the rabbinic tradition kept Hanukkah and forgot the book, preserving the festival's story in the Talmud's brief account of the miracle of the oil (Shabbat 21b) rather than in the Maccabees' own chronicle, a striking case of a community keeping the holiday and dropping the history. The church received both books in its Greek Bible: Jerome doubted them, Augustine defended them ("the books of the Maccabees, which not the Jews but the church holds as canonical"), the Western church read them for a millennium, and the Reformation divided over them, with 2 Maccabees 12's prayer for the dead becoming a flashpoint, cited for purgatory by Catholic theologians and rejected by the Reformers, who removed the book to the Apocrypha in part because of it, so that the Council of Trent's definition of the deuterocanon (1546) was, among other things, a ruling on this chapter. Today 1 and 2 Maccabees stand in the Catholic and Orthodox Old Testaments and in the Protestant Apocrypha; the Orthodox also receive 3 Maccabees (a Ptolemaic-era deliverance story, unrelated to the Maccabees) and print 4 Maccabees (a philosophical sermon on the martyrs' reason mastering their passions, whose vocabulary of the martyrs' deaths as "a ransom for the sin of our nation" and "an atoning sacrifice," 17:21-22, stands closer to the New Testament's than any other text of the period) as an appendix.

How the synagogue reads it

the synagogue kept the festival and let the books go. Hanukkah is observed to this day with the eight-branched lamp, the recited Al ha-Nissim ("for the miracles... in the days of Mattathias") in the daily prayers, and the Talmud's story of the single cruse of oil that burned eight days (Shabbat 21b), a legend the Maccabean books do not contain, while the books themselves, never Hebrew Scripture, survived only in the church's Greek. The rabbis' cooling on the Hasmoneans, the dynasty that became Sadducean and Hellenized in its turn, explains the selection: the miracle was God's and could be kept; the dynasty's chronicle was the Hasmoneans' and could be dropped. The martyrs, however, entered Jewish memory by another door: the story of "Hannah and her seven sons" (the mother is unnamed in 2 Maccabees; later tradition named her) is told in the Talmud and the midrash, and the mother's speech from creation to resurrection became a Jewish as well as a Christian text of consolation.

In plain terms: 1 Maccabees (Hebrew original lost, c. 100 BC) is the sober chronicle of the revolt: the Hellenizers, the abomination on the altar, Mattathias at Modein, Judas the Hammer, the rededication of 164 BC that founded Hanukkah, and the Hasmonean crown, held "until a trustworthy prophet should arise." Jesus kept its festival in Solomon's colonnade (John 10:22), and its prophetless-age laments are the era's own testimony to the silence. 2 Maccabees (Greek, c. 124 BC) is the moving one: Eleazar on the rack, the mother and her seven sons confessing resurrection, and Judas's sin offering for the dead, the text Trent and the Reformers fought over. Hebrews 11 places the martyrs, "tortured, refusing release, that they might rise to a better life," in the roll with Abraham. Off the Hebrew shelf as post-prophetic, dynastic, and (2 Maccabees) Greek; the synagogue kept the festival and dropped the books; the church kept both, and divided over one chapter.

The Deuterocanon, Continued

The Tales: Tobit, Judith, and the Additions

Tobit: the Angel on the Road, and the Golden Rule

Tobit is a story, the Second Temple period's most beloved, and it survives in Greek in two forms (a shorter and a longer), in Latin, and, since Qumran, in four Aramaic and one Hebrew manuscript, settling the old question of its original language in favor of Aramaic and its date around 225-175 BC, before the Maccabean crisis, probably in the eastern diaspora, whose life it depicts. The tale: Tobit, a righteous Israelite of Naphtali exiled to Nineveh, who tithes, gives alms, and buries the dead at risk of his life, is blinded by sparrow droppings while sleeping in his courtyard, quarrels with his wife, and prays for death; the same day, in Media, his kinswoman Sarah, whose seven bridegrooms have each been killed on the wedding night by the demon Asmodeus, prays for death too; God hears both prayers and sends the angel Raphael, disguised as a kinsman named Azariah, to guide Tobit's son Tobias on a journey to recover a deposit of silver, during which a great fish leaps from the Tigris (its heart, liver, and gall to be kept as medicine), Tobias marries Sarah and drives off the demon by burning the fish's organs, the deposit is recovered, Tobit's blindness is cured with the gall, and Raphael reveals himself ("I am Raphael, one of the seven angels who stand ready and enter before the glory of the Lord") and ascends, after which Tobit sings a hymn to Jerusalem's future glory and dies at a great age, having lived to see Nineveh's fall as Nahum foretold. Beneath the adventure runs a manual of diaspora piety: prayer, fasting, almsgiving ("almsgiving delivers from death and keeps you from going into the darkness," 4:10; "it is better to give alms than to lay up gold," 12:8), burial of the dead, marriage within the people, honor to parents, and the theology of the hidden God who hears the simultaneous prayers of a blind man in Nineveh and a shamed girl in Ecbatana and answers both with one journey, Providence wearing the disguise of a traveling companion.

Sealed in the New Testament the golden rule in its negative form, "And what you hate, do not do to anyone" (Tobit 4:15), is the earliest Jewish statement of the maxim Hillel would give a generation before Jesus ("what is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor; that is the whole Torah") and Jesus would state positively in the Sermon on the Mount ("whatever you wish that others would do to you, do also to them, for this is the Law and the Prophets," Matthew 7:12), the positive form being Jesus's own, the negative form Tobit's, two centuries earlier. Tobit's almsgiving theology ("almsgiving delivers from death," "lay up treasure for yourself against the day of necessity," 4:9-10) stands behind the New Testament's "lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven" and Luke's "give as alms those things that are within, and behold, everything is clean for you" (11:41), and its trio of prayer, fasting, and almsgiving ("prayer with fasting is good, and almsgiving with righteousness," 12:8) is exactly the trio the Sermon on the Mount audits (Matthew 6:1-18). Raphael's self-revelation as one of "the seven angels who stand ready and enter before the glory of the Lord" (12:15) is the background of Revelation's "seven spirits who are before his throne" and "the seven angels who stand before God" (1:4; 8:2). And the book's prayer-and-answer structure, two prayers heard in one moment and answered by one providence, is the theology of Esther and Ruth with an angel added. No quotation; several shared traditions of the first importance for the Gospels' ethics.

Why it is not on the shelf: it is a tale, set in the Assyrian exile and written five centuries later, with historical errors the rabbis and the fathers both noticed (Tobit's age at the Assyrian deportation, the geography of Media), its angelology and demonology (Asmodeus, the fish-gall cure) belonging to the folk-religion of the diaspora rather than to the prophetic tradition, and its language Aramaic (the Hebrew manuscript at Qumran is likely a translation). The synagogue never listed it; the church read it in Greek, Jerome translated it from an Aramaic copy in one night, he says, and doubted it; the Council of Trent received it; it stands in the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles and the Protestant Apocrypha, where Raphael's road and the fish have furnished Christian art for a thousand years.

In plain terms: Tobit (Aramaic, c. 225-175 BC, four Qumran copies) is the diaspora's favorite story: the righteous blind man in Nineveh and the shamed girl in Media pray for death the same day, and God sends Raphael in disguise to answer both with one journey, a fish, a wedding, and a cure. Beneath it runs a manual of piety: alms, prayer, fasting, burial, and the negative golden rule ("what you hate, do not do to anyone"), Hillel's form two centuries early and the Sermon on the Mount's positive form's ancestor. Its prayer-fasting-alms trio is Matthew 6's, its seven angels before the throne are Revelation's. Off the shelf as a late tale with historical slips and folk demonology; in the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles and the Apocrypha.

Judith: the Widow Who Cut Off the Head

Judith is a historical novel that announces its fictionality in its first sentence ("Nebuchadnezzar, who ruled over the Assyrians in the great city of Nineveh"), Nebuchadnezzar being king of Babylon, not Assyria, and the book's geography and chronology being impossible on purpose, the author signaling that this is a parable of deliverance rather than a chronicle. Written in Hebrew (lost; the Greek survives) probably in the Hasmonean period, around 150-100 BC, it tells how Nebuchadnezzar's general Holofernes marches on Judea after the return from exile (an anachronism), besieges the fictional town of Bethulia, and cuts off its water, and how, when the elders resolve to surrender in five days if God does not act, the beautiful and devout widow Judith rebukes them for testing God ("who are you to put God to the test today?"), prays, adorns herself, enters the enemy camp with her maid, captivates Holofernes over four days, and on the fourth night, when he lies drunk, takes his sword and "struck his neck twice with all her might, and cut off his head," carries it back in her food bag, hangs it on the wall, and leads Israel to rout the leaderless army, singing a victory hymn in the manner of Miriam and Deborah, and living as an honored widow to a hundred and five, never remarrying, "and no one ever again spread terror among the Israelites during the lifetime of Judith." Its readers, in the Maccabean generation, heard in it their own crisis in costume: a pagan tyrant demanding to be worshiped as a god, a small people at the temple's gate, deliverance through a single faithful hand, and the theology that the book states through Judith's mouth, "the Lord Almighty will defend us... by my hand," God's strength in the weakest instrument, the woman with the sword completing the line of Jael and Deborah.

Sealed in the New Testament there is no citation, and the echoes are of type: the widow as the model of devout Israel (Judith's fasting, prayer, and sackcloth beneath her finery anticipate Anna in the temple, "a widow until she was eighty-four... worshiping with fasting and prayer night and day," Luke 2:37); Judith's rebuke of testing God, "do not try to bind the purposes of the Lord our God," in the vocabulary Jesus used against the tempter; and the victory of the weak instrument, which is the theme Paul states as "God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong" (1 Corinthians 1:27). Clement of Rome, writing to Corinth around AD 96, cites Judith alongside Esther as an example of women's courage, the earliest Christian reference to the book.

Why it is not on the shelf: it is fiction with a Nebuchadnezzar of Assyria, and the rabbis, who knew the Judith story (a version is told in the medieval midrashim and attached to Hanukkah), never treated the book as Scripture; its Hebrew was lost and its Greek was the church's; Jerome translated it reluctantly and doubted it; Trent received it. It stands in the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles and the Protestant Apocrypha, and in the paintings of Caravaggio, Artemisia Gentileschi, and Klimt, where the sword and the head have never left the Western imagination.

In plain terms: Judith (Greek, from a lost Hebrew, c. 150-100 BC) announces itself as parable in its first line ("Nebuchadnezzar... of the Assyrians") and tells how a devout widow enters the enemy camp, beheads the general with his own sword, and delivers Israel: the Maccabean crisis in costume, and God's strength in the weakest hand, Jael and Deborah completed. No New Testament citation; Judith's fasting widowhood anticipates Anna, her "do not test God" is the tempter's answer, and her weak-instrument victory is Paul's. Off the shelf as fiction; the rabbis knew the story, not the book; Catholic and Orthodox Scripture, Protestant Apocrypha.

The Additions to Esther and to Daniel

The Greek Bible's Esther is a third longer than the Hebrew, and the additions are the Greek translators' answer to the book's famous silence: Mordecai's dream and its interpretation, the full texts of the decrees, and above all the prayers of Mordecai and of Esther before she goes to the king ("O my Lord, you only are our king; help me, who am alone and have no helper but you... save us by your hand"), which put the name of God into a book that never says it (the Greek Esther names God more than fifty times), and Esther's confession that she loathes the crown she wears and has not eaten at Haman's table, the Greek making explicit the piety the Hebrew leaves the reader to infer. Composed in Greek between the second century BC and the first century AD (the colophon dates the translation's arrival in Egypt to a Ptolemy's reign, probably 114 or 78 BC), the additions were Jerome's clearest case: he printed them at the end of his Esther as material "not found in the Hebrew," and Protestant Bibles follow him, while the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles read the longer Esther entire. The additions to Daniel are three: the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Young Men, inserted into the furnace scene of Daniel 3, where the three sing in the flames a hymn that calls on every creature to bless the Lord ("Bless the Lord, sun and moon... bless the Lord, all you works of the Lord"), the canticle the church has sung as the Benedicite since its earliest centuries, Psalm 148's roll-call in the furnace; Susanna, the story of the virtuous wife falsely accused of adultery by two elders whom the young Daniel exposes by cross-examining them separately ("under what tree?"), the origin of the detective story and of the proverb about cross-examination this series met in Proverbs 18:17; and Bel and the Dragon, two satires in which Daniel exposes the priests of Bel by scattering ashes on the temple floor to catch their footprints and kills a living dragon the Babylonians worship by feeding it pitch and hair, and is thrown a second time to the lions, where the prophet Habakkuk is carried by the hair to Babylon to feed him. Written in Greek (Susanna may rest on a Semitic original) in the second or first century BC, the additions circulated as parts of Daniel in every Greek and Latin Bible; Jerome kept them with an obelus (the sign for doubtful text); the Reformers moved them to the Apocrypha; Trent received them.

Sealed in the New Testament none of the additions is cited. The Song of the Three shares its creation-roll-call with Psalm 148 and Revelation's "every creature in heaven and on earth" praising (5:13); Susanna's separated-witness cross-examination is the principle behind "every charge must be established by the evidence of two or three witnesses" and its abuse; and the additions to Esther supply, as the Hebrew does not, the fasting-and-prayer piety that the Christian reader of Esther 4 assumes. The additions matter less for their echoes than for what they show: how Greek-speaking Jews read the books they translated, filling Esther's silence with prayer and Daniel's furnace with song, and how the church, receiving the Greek, received the readings with the text.

In plain terms: The Greek Esther adds a third to the Hebrew: Mordecai's dream, the decrees, and the prayers of Mordecai and Esther that put God's name into the book that never says it. The Greek Daniel adds three pieces: the Song of the Three in the furnace (the church's Benedicite, Psalm 148 in the flames), Susanna (the cross-examined elders, the first detective story), and Bel and the Dragon (Daniel's satire on idols). Greek, second-first century BC; Jerome marked them as absent from the Hebrew, Protestants moved them to the Apocrypha, Catholics and Orthodox read them within the books. Not cited in the New Testament; valuable for showing how Greek-speaking Jews read the Hebrew, filling silence with prayer and furnace with song.

The Deuterocanon, Continued

The Wisdom of the Era: Ben Sira and the Wisdom of Solomon

Ben Sira (Sirach, Ecclesiasticus): the Sage of Jerusalem

Ben Sira is the one book on this shelf whose author signs his name: "Jesus son of Eleazar son of Sirach of Jerusalem" (50:27), a scribe and teacher who kept a house of instruction in the city around 190-175 BC, in the last calm before Antiochus, and whose grandson, emigrating to Egypt in 132 BC, translated the book into Greek and wrote the prologue that attests the three shelves of the Hebrew canon (Part One of the orientation page). The Hebrew was known to the rabbis, quoted in the Talmud, and then lost for a millennium, until 1896, when fragments of a medieval Hebrew copy surfaced in the Cairo Genizah, the storeroom of a synagogue, and 1964, when a Hebrew scroll of Ben Sira was excavated at Masada in the very stratum of the Zealots' last stand, so that two-thirds of the Hebrew text is now recovered, and the book stands as a rare case of a lost original returned by the ground. It is the longest wisdom book of Israel's tradition, fifty-one chapters in the manner of Proverbs, two-line sayings and short essays on every subject a sage taught: the fear of the Lord as wisdom's root and crown (1:14-20); friendship ("a faithful friend is a sturdy shelter; he who finds one finds a treasure," 6:14); speech and silence; wealth and the poor; parents ("honor your father... kindness to a father will not be forgotten, and against your sins it will be credited to you," 3:14); physicians ("honor physicians, for the Lord created them," 38:1, the sage disagreeing with Asa's epitaph); the scribe's vocation against the artisan's (38:24-39:11, the famous passage on leisure as the condition of learning); and above all the identification the book makes its centerpiece: Wisdom, personified as in Proverbs 8, comes forth from the Most High, seeks a resting place among all peoples, and is commanded to dwell in Israel, "in the holy tabernacle I ministered before him, and so I was established in Zion," and then the sage names her: "All this is the book of the covenant of the Most High God, the law that Moses commanded us" (24:23), the preexistent Wisdom of Proverbs 8 identified with the Torah, the identification the rabbis would make the foundation of their theology and the church would answer with the Word made flesh (Volume Six's panel on Proverbs 8 rests on this text). The book closes with the "praise of famous men" (44-50), a roll of Israel's heroes from Enoch to the high priest Simon, whose liturgy in the temple the sage describes as an eyewitness ("How glorious he was... like the morning star among the clouds, like the moon when it is full"), the last portrait of the first-temple-style worship before the Maccabean storm, and with an autobiographical psalm and an invitation.

Sealed in the New Testament Ben Sira is the era's ethical handbook, and its parallels with the Gospels and James are dense enough that scholars have collected them by the dozen; the volume gives the strongest. The invitation at the book's close, "Draw near to me, you who are untaught, and lodge in my school... Put your neck under the yoke, and let your souls receive instruction; it is to be found close by. See with your eyes that I have labored little and found for myself much rest" (51:23-27), is the closest pre-Christian parallel to the Lord's "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me... and you will find rest for your souls" (Matthew 11:28-29), the yoke of wisdom offered by the sage and the yoke of Christ offered by Wisdom incarnate, with the difference the volume marks: Ben Sira invites to his school, Jesus to himself. The forgiveness teaching, "Forgive your neighbor the wrong he has done, and then your sins will be pardoned when you pray. Does a man harbor anger against another, and yet seek for healing from the Lord?" (28:2-4), is the Lord's Prayer's "forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors" and the parable of the unforgiving servant in the sage's voice. "Be quick to hear, and with patience give answer" (5:11) is James's "let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak" (1:19); "do not say, 'Because of the Lord I left the right way'... he has not commanded anyone to be ungodly" (15:11-20) is James's "let no one say when he is tempted, 'I am being tempted by God'" (1:13); and the sage's warnings on the tongue (chapters 19-20, 28) and on the rich man's oppression of the poor (13:3-4, 34:20-22) supply James's own themes. Luke's "when you give a banquet, invite the poor" and the sage's counsel on almsgiving ("store up almsgiving in your treasury, and it will rescue you from all affliction," 29:12) share a world; the rich fool of Luke 12 is the sage's "there is a man who is rich through his diligence and self-denial, and this is the reward allotted to him: when he says, 'I have found rest, and now I shall enjoy my goods!' he does not know how much time will pass until he leaves them to others and dies" (11:18-19), nearly the parable in prose. No quotation; a dozen close parallels, the densest ethical overlap of any book in this volume with the New Testament.

Why it is not on the shelf: the era, first: Ben Sira wrote around 180 BC, and his grandson's prologue, which distinguishes "the Law and the Prophets and the other books" from his grandfather's work, is itself the witness that the sage did not claim to add to them. The rabbis quoted him, sometimes with the formula reserved for Scripture, and then drew the line: the Talmud lists Ben Sira among the "outside books" one may not read publicly as Scripture (Sanhedrin 100b) while continuing to cite his sayings, the clearest rabbinic case of a book honored and excluded at once. The church received it in Greek under the name "Ecclesiasticus," the church book, so called, on one account, because it was the book read to catechumens for instruction; Jerome doubted it; Augustine defended it; Trent received it. It stands in the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles and the Protestant Apocrypha, and its "Now thank we all our God" (50:22-24) became, through a German hymn, one of the church's great hymns of thanksgiving.

How the synagogue reads it

Ben Sira is the "outside book" par excellence, and the rabbinic treatment is a study in nuance. The Talmud quotes him some dozens of times, occasionally introducing a saying with "as it is written," the formula for Scripture, and preserves sayings not in the Greek; the same Talmud records Rabbi Joseph's ruling that the book may not be read, and Rabbi Akiva's warning about those who read the outside books; and the resolution offered is that Ben Sira's good sayings may be taught and cited while the book as a whole is not Scripture. The rediscovered Hebrew confirmed the rabbis' familiarity: the Genizah fragments carry marginal notes and variants showing the book was copied and studied in medieval synagogue circles. The synagogue's position, then, was neither rejection nor reception but the middle category this volume has needed a name for throughout: wisdom worth quoting from a book not worth canonizing, which is close to the church's own "read for instruction, not for doctrine."

In plain terms: Ben Sira (Jerusalem, c. 180 BC, Hebrew, translated by his grandson in 132 BC) is the era's longest wisdom book and its ethical handbook: friendship, speech, the poor, physicians, the scribe's leisure, the praise of famous men, and the centerpiece that identifies Proverbs 8's Wisdom with the Torah, the text behind the rabbis' theology and Volume Six's panel. Lost in Hebrew for a millennium, returned by a Cairo storeroom and Masada. The densest New Testament overlap of any book here: the yoke and rest of Matthew 11, the Lord's Prayer's forgiveness clause, James's quick-to-hear and not-tempted-by-God, Luke's rich fool nearly in prose. Off the shelf by its own grandson's distinction and the Talmud's ruling (quoted with Scripture's formula, listed among the outside books), "Ecclesiasticus" in the church, Catholic and Orthodox Scripture, Protestant Apocrypha.

The Wisdom of Solomon: the Righteous Man and the Argument of Romans 1

The Wisdom of Solomon is a Greek book, composed in Greek, in Alexandria, by a Jew steeped in Greek philosophy and the Septuagint, probably in the first century BC (some place it as late as the reign of Caligula, c. AD 40, reading its persecution passages as responses to the Alexandrian pogrom of 38), under the persona of Solomon, who is never named but unmistakably speaks ("I also am mortal... I was nursed with care in swaddling cloths... therefore I prayed, and understanding was given me," 7:1-7). It is the most philosophically ambitious book of the period, in three movements. The first (1-6) is an address to rulers on righteousness and immortality, containing the passage the New Testament's passion narratives echo: the wicked reason that life is short and death final, so "let us oppress the righteous poor man... Let us lie in wait for the righteous man, because he is inconvenient to us and opposes our actions; he reproaches us for sins against the law... He professes to have knowledge of God, and calls himself a child of the Lord... he calls the last end of the righteous happy, and boasts that God is his father. Let us see if his words are true, and let us test what will happen at the end of his life; for if the righteous man is God's son, he will help him, and will deliver him from the hand of his adversaries. Let us test him with insult and torture... Let us condemn him to a shameful death, for, according to what he says, he will be protected" (2:10-20), the persecutors' logic laid bare, followed by the book's answer, which is the fullest Jewish statement of the soul's immortality before the New Testament: "the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and no torment will ever touch them. In the eyes of the foolish they seemed to have died... but they are at peace... their hope is full of immortality" (3:1-4), and the vindication scene in which the righteous man stands "with great confidence in the presence of those who have oppressed him," and they say, "This is the man whom we once held in derision... We fools! We thought that his life was madness and that his end was without honor. Why has he been numbered among the sons of God?" (5:1-5). The second movement (7-9) is Solomon's praise of Wisdom, whom he sought as a bride, and the book's famous description of her: "a breath of the power of God, and a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty... a reflection of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God, and an image of his goodness... she renews all things; in every generation she passes into holy souls and makes them friends of God, and prophets" (7:25-27), Proverbs 8's craftsman described in the vocabulary of Greek philosophy (emanation, reflection, image), with Solomon's prayer for her as the climax ("give me the wisdom that sits by your throne," 9:4, "who was present when you made the world," 9:9). The third (10-19) is a meditation on Wisdom's guidance of history from Adam to the exodus, with the long digression the volume flags as the book's most consequential passage for the New Testament: the origin and folly of idolatry (13-15), which argues that the natural world should have led the nations to its Maker ("from the greatness and beauty of created things comes a corresponding perception of their Creator," 13:5), that they were "without excuse" for worshiping the works instead (13:8), that idolatry began in grief and in kings' images and became "the beginning and cause and end of every evil" (14:27), and that from it flowed a catalog of vices, sexual perversion, murder, theft, deceit, "all is a raging riot of blood and murder" (14:22-26), the whole argued with a philosopher's structure.

Sealed in the New Testament the echoes here are the most extensive of any book in this volume after Ben Sira, and two are of the first importance. First, Romans 1:18-32 walks the argument of Wisdom 13-14 step by step: creation as the revelation that leaves the nations "without excuse" (Romans 1:20; Wisdom 13:8 uses the same Greek idea), the exchange of the Creator's glory for images of "mortal man and birds and animals and creeping things" (Romans 1:23; Wisdom 13:10-14), the darkening of the mind that follows, and idolatry as the root from which the vice-list grows (Romans 1:24-32; Wisdom 14:22-31), the sequence, the categories, and in places the vocabulary matching so closely that the consensus of scholarship holds Paul knew the book, and the point of the parallel is Paul's turn: Wisdom's author, having indicted the Gentiles, exempts Israel ("but you, our God, are kind and true," 15:1); Paul, having walked the same indictment, turns it on the Jew in Romans 2 ("Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges"), the apostle borrowing the argument and refusing its exemption, all under sin. Second, the mockery at the cross: "He trusts in God; let God deliver him now, if he desires him. For he said, 'I am the Son of God'" (Matthew 27:43) echoes Psalm 22:8 in its first half and Wisdom 2:18 in its second ("if the righteous man is God's son, he will help him, and will deliver him"), the Gospel's mockers reasoning as Wisdom's persecutors reasoned, testing the righteous man's sonship by whether God rescues him, and the chief priests, on this reading, speaking not only Psalm 22's line but Wisdom 2's, the righteous sufferer of Wisdom standing behind Matthew's Jesus as Isaiah's Servant stands behind him in the other Gospels. Beyond these two: Wisdom's description of Wisdom as "a reflection of eternal light... an image of his goodness" (7:26) is the source-language of Hebrews 1:3 ("the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature") and Colossians 1:15 ("the image of the invisible God"), the christological titles drawn from the sapiential vocabulary; "God did not make death... for he created all things so that they might exist... but through the devil's envy death entered the world" (1:13-14; 2:23-24) is the Jewish source of the New Testament's "sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin" and of the identification of Eden's serpent with the devil, made explicit here for the first time in Jewish literature; the armor of God ("he will take his zeal as his whole armor... he will put on righteousness as a breastplate, and wear impartial justice as a helmet; he will take holiness as an invincible shield," 5:17-20) is Isaiah 59's armor expanded into the panoply Ephesians 6 issues to the church; and the manna as "the food of angels" (16:20) and the "leaping" of the Word from heaven into the doomed land (18:14-16, "your all-powerful word leaped from heaven, from the royal throne, into the midst of the land that was doomed, a stern warrior") supplied the church's Christmas liturgy its language and John's prologue a resonance. Grading: no quotation; one sustained structural dependence (Romans 1); one probable citation-in-mockery (Matthew 27); several near-certain sources of New Testament vocabulary.

Why it is not on the shelf: it is Greek, Alexandrian, and philosophical, composed a thousand years after the king in whose voice it speaks, and its doctrine of the soul (preexistent, imprisoned in the body, immortal by nature) is Plato's more than Moses's; the Hebrew canon had no place for a Greek Solomon, and the rabbis never mention it. The church loved it: it is cited by the fathers more than any other deuterocanonical book, its Wisdom-christology fed the christology of the councils, Augustine defended it against the Hebrew-only line, Jerome grudged it, and Trent received it. It stands in the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles and the Protestant Apocrypha, and its third chapter ("the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God") is read at Christian funerals of every tradition, including many that do not call it Scripture.

In plain terms: Wisdom of Solomon (Greek, Alexandria, first century BC, in Solomon's unnamed voice) is the era's philosophical summit: the persecutors' plot against the righteous man who "calls himself God's son" ("let us test whether God will deliver him"), the souls of the righteous "in the hand of God," Wisdom as "reflection of eternal light" and "image of his goodness," and the great argument that idolatry, inexcusable before creation's witness, is the root of every vice. Romans 1 walks that argument step by step and then refuses its exemption for the Jew; Matthew 27's mockers reason as Wisdom 2's persecutors do; Hebrews 1:3 and Colossians 1:15 speak its vocabulary; and "through the devil's envy death entered the world" first names Eden's serpent as the devil. Off the Hebrew shelf as Greek, late, and Platonic in its soul-doctrine; the church's favorite deuterocanonical book, Catholic and Orthodox Scripture, Protestant Apocrypha, and read at funerals everywhere.

The Voices of the Exile Revisited: Baruch, the Letter, the Prayer of Manasseh, and the Orthodox Extras

Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah

Baruch presents itself as written by Jeremiah's scribe in Babylon five years after Jerusalem's fall and read to the exiles, and is in fact a composite of three or four pieces from the second or first century BC, probably translated from Hebrew: a confession of the nation's sin in the manner of Daniel 9 and Nehemiah 9 (1:15-3:8, "righteousness belongs to the Lord our God, but confusion of face to us"), a wisdom poem identifying Wisdom with the Torah in Ben Sira's manner (3:9-4:4, "she is the book of the commandments of God, and the law that endures forever... Afterward she appeared on earth and lived with humankind," 3:37-4:1, the last line the fathers read as a prophecy of the incarnation), and a consolation of Jerusalem in Isaiah 40-66's voice (4:5-5:9, "Arise, O Jerusalem, stand upon the height and look toward the east... for God will lead Israel with joy, in the light of his glory"). The Letter of Jeremiah, attached to Baruch as its sixth chapter in the Latin tradition, is a satire on idols in the manner of Isaiah 44 and Psalm 115, written probably in the fourth or third century BC (a Greek fragment was found at Qumran), warning the exiles that the gods of Babylon "cannot save themselves from rust and corrosion," that cats and bats sit on them, and that "they are like a scarecrow in a cucumber bed, which guards nothing" (v. 70, Jeremiah 10:5's image reused). Neither is cited in the New Testament; Baruch's confession stands in the same tradition as the Lord's Prayer's plural petitions, and its Wisdom-appearing-on-earth line gave the church a Christmas text. Both are Greek in survival, post-prophetic in origin, and pseudonymous; the rabbis never mention Baruch's book (though other Baruch apocalypses circulated); the church read them with Jeremiah; Trent received them; they stand in the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles and the Apocrypha.

The Prayer of Manasseh, Psalm 151, and 1 Esdras

The Prayer of Manasseh is the prayer 2 Chronicles 33 mentions and does not quote: fifteen verses of penitence composed in Greek (or from a Hebrew original) in the second or first century BC to supply what the Chronicler's readers wished they had, the worst king's actual words ("I have sinned beyond the number of the sand of the sea... I am weighed down with many an iron fetter... and now I bend the knee of my heart, beseeching you for your kindness"), one of the finest penitential prayers of the era, read in the Orthodox Great Compline, printed in the Vulgate's appendix and the Apocrypha, canonical in the Orthodox churches and not in the Catholic. Psalm 151 is a short autobiographical psalm of David ("I was small among my brothers... my hands made a harp... he sent his messenger and took me from my father's sheep"), Greek in the Septuagint and, since Qumran, Hebrew in the great Psalms scroll from Cave 11 (which held it as two psalms), canonical in the Orthodox churches. And 1 Esdras (called 3 Ezra in the Vulgate) is a Greek retelling of 2 Chronicles 35 through Nehemiah 8, the Josiah-to-Ezra story, with one addition found nowhere else: the debate of the three bodyguards before Darius on what is strongest (wine, the king, women, or truth), won by Zerubbabel's speech for truth ("Great is truth, and strongest of all," 4:41, magna est veritas et praevalet in the Latin the West remembered), which secures the king's permission to rebuild; Josephus used 1 Esdras as his source for the period, the Greek fathers cited it, the Orthodox receive it, the Catholic Church printed it in an appendix, and the Protestant Apocrypha includes it. None of the three is cited in the New Testament. The Prayer of Manasseh completes the story Volume Eight told; Psalm 151 shows the Psalter's edges still soft at Qumran; and 1 Esdras's "great is truth" shows how the return was remembered in the Greek-speaking diaspora.

In plain terms: Baruch (composite, second-first century BC) offers a Daniel 9-style confession, a Wisdom-is-Torah poem whose "afterward she appeared on earth" the fathers read of the incarnation, and an Isaiah-voiced consolation; the Letter of Jeremiah (fourth-third century BC) is an idol-satire, "a scarecrow in a cucumber bed." The Prayer of Manasseh supplies the words Chronicles omitted ("I bend the knee of my heart"); Psalm 151 is David's autobiographical psalm, Greek and Qumran-Hebrew; 1 Esdras retells the return with the bodyguards' debate and "great is truth, and strongest of all." None cited in the New Testament; all Greek in survival and post-prophetic; Catholic and Orthodox Scripture (with the last three Orthodox only), Protestant Apocrypha.

The Visionaries: the Psalms of Solomon, 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch, and the Martyrdom of Isaiah

The Psalms of Solomon: the Lord Messiah, a Generation Before Jesus

The Psalms of Solomon are eighteen psalms in the manner of the Psalter, composed in Hebrew (lost) in Jerusalem in the decades after Pompey's conquest of the city in 63 BC (the Roman general's siege, his entry into the holy of holies, and his death in Egypt in 48 BC are described in Psalms 2 and 8 with an eyewitness's specificity), surviving in Greek and Syriac, and attributed to Solomon by a later hand. They are the Pharisees' hymnbook, or something very near it: the psalms condemn the Hasmonean priest-kings as usurpers who "set up a monarchy in place of what was promised" and defiled the sanctuary, read Pompey's conquest as God's judgment on them, distinguish "the devout" (the psalmists' own party) from "the sinners" (the Sadducean establishment), teach the resurrection of the righteous and the destruction of the wicked, and affirm free will and divine discipline in terms the Gospels' Pharisees would own. And in their seventeenth and eighteenth psalms they give the fullest description of the awaited Messiah in any Jewish text before the New Testament, and the volume prints its heart: "See, Lord, and raise up for them their king, the son of David, to rule over your servant Israel in the time known to you, O God. Undergird him with the strength to destroy the unrighteous rulers, to purge Jerusalem from the Gentiles who trample her to destruction... He will gather a holy people whom he will lead in righteousness... He will not tolerate unrighteousness even to pause among them... He will judge peoples and nations in the wisdom of his righteousness... And he will be a righteous king over them, taught by God. There will be no unrighteousness among them in his days, for all shall be holy, and their king shall be the Lord Messiah" (17:21-32), and again, "the Lord himself is his king... he will be compassionate to all the nations who reverently stand before him... he will not rely on horse and rider and bow, nor will he collect gold and silver for war... for he will strike the earth with the word of his mouth forever; he will bless the Lord's people with wisdom and happiness. And he himself will be free from sin, in order to rule a great people... This is the beauty of the king of Israel which God knew, to raise him over the house of Israel to discipline it. His words will be purer than the finest gold... His words will be as the words of the holy ones" (17:33-43). The volume marks what the portrait contains and what it lacks: a Davidic king, raised by God, sinless, taught by God, striking with the word of his mouth (Isaiah 11:4), refusing horses and gold (Deuteronomy 17 and Zechariah 9), compassionate to the reverent nations, gathering a holy people, and titled, in the Greek, christos kyrios, "Lord Messiah" or "the Lord's Anointed," the phrase Luke's angel will use of a child in a manger ("a Savior, who is Christ the Lord," Luke 2:11, the same two words); and no suffering, no death, no priesthood, no atonement, the Messiah of the Psalms of Solomon being the conquering, purifying, ruling son of David whom the crowds on Palm Sunday expected and whom the Gospels present as arriving on a donkey to die.

Sealed in the New Testament no quotation, and a relationship of the first importance for reading the Gospels: the Psalms of Solomon are the best evidence for what "the Christ" meant in Jerusalem in the generation before Jesus, and the New Testament's every surprise, the Messiah who is baptized, tempted, suffers, is crucified, and rises, is a surprise measured against exactly this expectation. Luke's "Christ the Lord" (2:11) and "the Lord's Christ" (2:26, Simeon's promise) carry the Psalms' title; the Palm Sunday crowd's "Blessed is the coming kingdom of our father David" (Mark 11:10) is Psalm 17's hope in a shout; the disciples' "we had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel" (Luke 24:21) and their post-resurrection "will you at this time restore the kingdom to Israel?" (Acts 1:6) are the Psalms' program still expected; and Jesus's refusal of the sword, his riding of the donkey, his "my kingdom is not of this world," and the whole Isaiah 53 reading of his death are, in the volume's judgment, the New Testament's deliberate revision of the portrait the Psalms of Solomon paint, the sinless Davidic king kept, the Gentile-purging conquest replaced by the cross and the Gentile mission. A reader who wants to feel the Gospels' originality should read Psalm of Solomon 17 first.

Why it is not on the shelf: pseudonymous (Solomon did not write about Pompey), first century BC, the hymnbook of a party, and lost in Hebrew; the rabbis do not mention it, the church preserved it in Greek (listed in some manuscripts of the Septuagint and in the fifth-century Codex Alexandrinus's table of contents), never canonized it, and let it drop until modern scholarship recovered it as the key to the Gospels' messianic background.

In plain terms: Eighteen psalms from Jerusalem after Pompey (63 BC), the Pharisees' hymnbook in all but name, condemning the Hasmoneans, teaching resurrection and free will, and painting the fullest pre-Christian portrait of the Messiah: the son of David, sinless, taught by God, striking with the word of his mouth, refusing horses and gold, purging Jerusalem of Gentiles, gathering a holy people, titled "Lord Messiah", Luke's very words at the manger. No suffering, no cross, no priesthood: the Messiah Palm Sunday expected and Good Friday revised. Never canonized; preserved in Greek by the church; the key to what "Christ" meant when the Gospels used the word.

4 Ezra (2 Esdras 3-14): the Man from the Sea, and the Twenty-Four Books

4 Ezra is the greatest Jewish apocalypse after Daniel, and the most anguished, written in Hebrew or Aramaic (lost) around AD 100, a generation after Rome burned the second temple, and set, by its author's design, in Babylon thirty years after the first temple's burning, so that the fictional Ezra of 557 BC voices the real grief of AD 100. It survives in Latin (as chapters 3-14 of the book the Vulgate calls 4 Esdras and English Apocryphas call 2 Esdras, with two Christian chapters added at each end) and in Syriac, Ethiopic, Arabic, and Armenian, from a lost Greek. Its form is seven visions. In the first three, Ezra argues with the angel Uriel about the problem Job and Habakkuk raised and the destruction of the temple made unbearable: why has God given the world to Babylon, whose sins exceed Zion's? Why was Israel, for whom the world was made, given to the nations? And why, if Adam's transgression left an "evil heart" in all his descendants, are the many condemned and the few saved? ("O Adam, what have you done? For though it was you who sinned, the fall was not yours alone, but ours also who are your descendants," 7:118, the doctrine of inherited sin stated in Jewish terms a generation after Paul.) Uriel's answers are Job's whirlwind in an angel's mouth (you cannot weigh fire or measure the wind; how then can you understand the Most High's ways?), and the book's honesty is that Ezra is not satisfied, the angel conceding only that God's mercy is greater than Ezra knows and the number of the saved fixed. The fourth vision is the turn: Ezra comforts a mourning woman who has lost her only son, and she is transformed before him into a city, Zion rebuilt, the consoler consoled. The fifth is the eagle from the sea, Rome (the eagle's wings and heads its emperors, the book says outright that this is "the fourth kingdom which appeared in a vision to your brother Daniel, but it was not explained to him as I now explain it," 12:11-12, an ancient reader identifying Daniel's fourth beast as Rome), rebuked and destroyed by a lion, the Messiah "from the posterity of David," who reproves the eagle for its injustice and ends it. The sixth is the man from the sea: "something like the figure of a man came up out of the heart of the sea... and that man flew with the clouds of heaven," gathering a multitude against him and destroying them with a stream of fire from his mouth, then calling to himself a peaceable multitude, the ten tribes returning, and the interpretation names him "my Son" whom the Most High has kept "for many ages" (13:1-52), Daniel 7's son of man read as a preexistent Messiah who comes on the clouds, judges the nations by the word of his mouth, and gathers Israel, and the volume flags the companion text in the same book: the Messiah "shall be revealed with those who are with him, and those who remain shall rejoice four hundred years. After those years my Son the Messiah shall die, and all who draw human breath" (7:28-29), a dying Messiah, not as atonement but as the end of the messianic age before the general resurrection and judgment, the only Jewish text of the period to say the Messiah dies. The seventh vision is the book's most famous for the history of the canon: Ezra, since the Scriptures were burned with the temple, is inspired to dictate them anew, and in forty days with five scribes produces ninety-four books, of which God commands, "Make public the twenty-four books that you wrote first, and let the worthy and the unworthy read them; but keep the seventy that were written last, in order to give them to the wise among your people" (14:45-46), the twenty-four public books being the Hebrew Bible's own count, attested here around AD 100 in a Jewish text, and the seventy "hidden" books being the apocalyptic literature (including this one) that the author held as a higher, esoteric revelation, the word "apocrypha," hidden, being born in this sentence.

Sealed in the New Testament 4 Ezra postdates most of the New Testament and cannot be its source; its importance is as the nearest Jewish contemporary of the Gospels and Paul, and the parallels show two communities reading Daniel and Genesis in the same generation. The man from the sea who flies with the clouds and judges with fire from his mouth is the Son of Man of the Gospels and Revelation ("from his mouth came a sharp two-edged sword," Revelation 1:16; 19:15) developed independently from Daniel 7; the eagle as Rome is Revelation's beast in another symbol, and the book's explicit reading of Daniel's fourth kingdom as Rome confirms the framework the New Testament assumes; the "evil heart" inherited from Adam is the closest Jewish parallel to Romans 5's "sin came into the world through one man," and the anguish over the few saved and the many lost is the pastoral shadow of "many are called, but few are chosen"; and the Christian additions (2 Esdras 1-2, 15-16, from the second and third centuries) show the church adopting the book, with the fifth-chapter "the Son of God" and the second chapter's white-robed multitude crowned by "the Son of God" being Christian readers' completion of the Jewish text. The twenty-four books of 14:45 are the volume's most valuable single datum for the orientation page's argument: a Jew around AD 100, writing an apocalypse he wished were Scripture, testifies that the public Bible was twenty-four books, and files his own work among the hidden seventy.

Why it is not on the shelf: it postdates the prophets by five centuries, is pseudonymous, and classes itself among the hidden books; the rabbis, who claimed Ezra's mantle, never mention it; the church read it in Latin and Syriac and Ethiopic, printed it in the Vulgate's appendix (the Council of Trent left it out of the deuterocanon), Luther and the English translators included it in the Apocrypha, and it is canonical only in the Ethiopian church (and, as 2 Esdras, in the Slavonic Orthodox appendix). It survives as the era's most honest theodicy and the source of the phrase "apocrypha."

In plain terms: 4 Ezra (c. AD 100, after Rome burned the temple, set in Babylon after the first burning) is Daniel's successor and Job's heir: seven visions in which Ezra argues with the angel Uriel about why God gave Zion to Rome, why Adam's "evil heart" is ours, and why the few are saved, and is answered with the whirlwind's humility and no satisfaction. The mourning woman becomes Zion; the eagle (Rome, named as Daniel's fourth beast) is ended by the lion, the Davidic Messiah; and the man from the sea flies with the clouds, judges with fire from his mouth, and gathers Israel, Daniel 7's son of man as preexistent Messiah, who, uniquely, dies after four hundred years. Ezra re-dictates the burned Scriptures: twenty-four public books (the Hebrew Bible's count, attested here) and seventy hidden, the birth of the word "apocrypha." Contemporary with the Gospels, not their source; canonical only in Ethiopia; in the Apocrypha as 2 Esdras.

2 Baruch: the Apocalypse of the Scribe

2 Baruch (the Syriac Apocalypse of Baruch) is 4 Ezra's twin, written in the same generation (c. AD 100) in Hebrew (lost), surviving in Syriac, set at the fall of the first temple and voiced by Jeremiah's scribe, and answering the same grief with more resolution: the temple's fall is explained as God's own act (the angels remove the sacred vessels and hide them in the earth before the Chaldeans enter, "that strangers may not boast that they have taken it"), the present age is a corruptible one soon to end, and the coming age brings the Messiah, the resurrection of the dead in bodies recognizable for judgment and then transformed into the splendor of the stars, and a renewed creation in which "the earth will yield its fruit ten thousandfold... and on one vine there will be a thousand branches, and each branch will produce a thousand clusters, and each cluster will produce a thousand grapes, and each grape will produce a cor of wine" (29:5, the famous millennial abundance that Papias, in the second century, attributed to Jesus himself as a saying about the kingdom, the one place where a New Testament-era Jewish apocalypse and the earliest church's oral memory of Jesus visibly overlap). Its clearest teaching is on Adam and free will, and it is the deliberate counterpart to 4 Ezra's despair: "Adam is therefore not the cause, except only for himself, but each of us has become our own Adam" (54:19), each generation responsible, the evil heart real and the will free, the theology of the Pharisees and later the rabbis against the darker inheritance 4 Ezra and, in his own way, Paul described. Its vision of the cloud from the sea with its twelve waters, dark and bright alternating, tells Israel's history to the Messiah's reign; its closing letter to the nine and a half tribes urges the diaspora to keep the law, "for we have nothing now except the Mighty One and his law" (85:3), the post-70 synagogue's whole program in one line. No New Testament citation; the "each of us his own Adam" line is the best Jewish counterpoint to Romans 5, and the resurrection-body passage (chapters 49-51, the dead raised as they were for recognition, then transformed) is the nearest Jewish parallel to 1 Corinthians 15's sown-in-corruption-raised-in-glory. Never canonical anywhere except that the Syriac church transmitted it; unknown to the rabbis; a witness to how Judaism rebuilt itself on the law after the temple, in the same decades the church built itself on the resurrection.

The Martyrdom (Ascension) of Isaiah: the Prophet Sawn in Two

Hebrews 11's roll of the faithful ends with the unnamed who "were stoned, they were sawn in two, they were killed with the sword" (11:37), and "sawn in two" points to a tradition the Hebrew Bible does not record: that the prophet Isaiah, in the reign of Manasseh, was hidden in a cedar tree and sawn in half inside it by the king's men. The tradition is Jewish and old: the Talmud tells it (Yevamot 49b, Manasseh sawing Isaiah in a cedar, with the prophet's own words about "unclean lips" cited against him at his trial), the Lives of the Prophets (first century AD) records it, and its fullest form is the Martyrdom of Isaiah, a Jewish work of the second or first century BC that survives as the first five chapters of a Christian composite called the Ascension of Isaiah (in Ethiopic, with Latin and Greek fragments), where a second-century Christian vision (Isaiah's ascent through the seven heavens to see the Beloved descend in secret and be crucified) was joined to the Jewish martyrdom. The Jewish core tells of the false prophet Belkira accusing Isaiah before Manasseh of prophesying Jerusalem's ruin and claiming to have seen God (Isaiah 6, against Exodus 33's "no one may see me and live"), of Isaiah's refusal to recant, and of his death by the wooden saw while "he neither cried aloud nor wept, but his lips spoke with the Holy Spirit until he was sawn in two." Sealed in the New Testament Hebrews 11:37's "sawn in two" is, on the consensus of ancient and modern readers, this tradition, the letter counting Isaiah's martyrdom among the acts of faith as it counted the Maccabean martyrs three verses earlier, both from outside the Hebrew canon, and Justin Martyr, Tertullian, Origen, and Jerome all knew the tradition and connected it to Hebrews; beyond it, the Christian Ascension's vision of the descent of the Beloved through the heavens, disguised at each level, to be born, crucified, and to ascend again, is an early Christian meditation in the vocabulary of Philippians 2's emptying and Colossians' hidden mystery. Why it is not on the shelf: the Jewish core is a pseudonymous legend of the second century BC, and the Christian expansion is a second-century vision; neither community canonized it, the rabbis kept the sawing as an aggadah, and the church kept it as the explanation of one verse of Hebrews, which is precisely its value.

In plain terms: 2 Baruch (c. AD 100, Syriac survival) is 4 Ezra's more consoled twin: the temple's vessels hidden by angels, the Messiah and a millennial abundance (the thousand-branched vine that Papias attributed to Jesus), the resurrection-body raised for recognition and then transformed (1 Corinthians 15's nearest parallel), and "each of us has become our own Adam" against 4 Ezra's despair, the post-70 synagogue's program: "we have nothing now except the Mighty One and his law." The Martyrdom of Isaiah (second-first century BC Jewish core inside a second-century Christian Ascension) tells the prophet sawn in two in a cedar under Manasseh, the tradition Hebrews 11:37 counts among the acts of faith and the Talmud tells as aggadah. Neither canonical anywhere.

The Library of the Dead Sea Community

The Discovery, the Community, and Why It Matters

In the winter of 1946-47 a Bedouin shepherd threw a stone into a cave above the northwest shore of the Dead Sea and heard pottery break, and the jars he found held the first of the Dead Sea Scrolls: some nine hundred manuscripts, most in fragments, recovered from eleven caves near the ruins of Qumran between 1947 and 1956, copied between roughly 250 BC and AD 68, when the Roman legions marching on Jerusalem destroyed the settlement. About a quarter are biblical, every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther, a thousand years older than any Hebrew manuscript previously known, and their testimony to the text's fidelity is Part Three of the orientation page. The rest fall in two groups: books this volume has already walked (Enoch, Jubilees, Tobit, Ben Sira, the Aramaic Levi behind the Testaments) and the community's own compositions, unknown to anyone for nineteen centuries, the rules, hymns, commentaries, calendars, and visions of a Jewish sect that had withdrawn to the desert to keep the law as it believed the law should be kept. The consensus identifies the community with the Essenes, the third Jewish party of the Gospels' era, described by Josephus, Philo, and Pliny (who places them "on the west shore of the Dead Sea, away from the coast," exactly here), a priestly movement that rejected the Hasmonean high priesthood as illegitimate, kept the solar calendar of Enoch and Jubilees, practiced community of property, daily immersions, and communal meals, and awaited the end of the age under a founder its texts call the Teacher of Righteousness. The library matters to this series for a reason its four earlier libraries cannot match: it is the only body of Jewish religious writing from the exact generation before Jesus preserved as it was, uncopied by later hands, unedited by church or synagogue, the sealed time-capsule of what one community of devout Jews expected, prayed, and read while John was in the wilderness and Jesus in Nazareth. Its compositions are not Scripture and never claimed to be; they are context, and the New Testament reads differently once they are known.

The Rules: the Community Rule and the Damascus Document

The Community Rule (1QS, the "Manual of Discipline," found nearly complete in Cave 1) is the sect's constitution: the covenant-entry ceremony with its blessings and curses in Deuteronomy's form; the Two Spirits doctrine, in which God has appointed for humanity "two spirits in which to walk until the time of his visitation: the spirits of truth and of falsehood," the sons of light and the sons of darkness, with the Prince of Light and the Angel of Darkness contending in every heart until the end (3:13-4:26); the rules of admission (a two-year probation, property merged, rank assigned annually), of the assembly, of the meal, and of discipline (penalties for interrupting, for lying about property, for laughing foolishly, for falling asleep in the assembly); and the community's self-understanding as the true temple in the desert, "a house of holiness for Israel and a foundation of the holy of holies for Aaron," offering "the offering of the lips" in place of the sacrifices its members would not offer in a defiled sanctuary, "to prepare in the wilderness the way of the Lord," the Isaiah 40:3 verse quoted as the community's charter (8:12-16). The Damascus Document (CD, known from two medieval copies found in the Cairo Genizah in 1896 and identified as the sect's when Qumran fragments surfaced) is the rule for the movement's members living in "camps" in the towns of Israel, married, with children and property, under stricter Sabbath and purity law than the Pharisees kept, and its opening is the sect's history: God "caused a root of planting to spring from Israel and Aaron" three hundred and ninety years after the exile, a generation that "groped for the way" for twenty years, "and God raised for them a Teacher of Righteousness to guide them in the way of his heart," opposed by "the Scoffer" or "the Man of Lies" who led the mainstream astray, the community founded, as it understood itself, by a persecuted teacher whose interpretation of the law and the prophets was the only true one.

Sealed in the New Testament the parallels are of context, and several are close. The Baptist's ministry "in the wilderness of Judea," announcing the kingdom, baptizing in the Jordan a few miles from Qumran, and described by all four Gospels with Isaiah 40:3, the very verse the Community Rule made its charter, has led many to ask whether John knew the community (Luke's note that he "was in the wilderness until the day of his public appearance," 1:80, has fed the speculation), and whatever the answer, the Gospels' "voice in the wilderness" landed in a Judea where a sect had been preparing the way in the wilderness on the same verse for a century. The sons of light and sons of darkness, the spirit of truth and the spirit of error, the light-and-darkness dualism that pervades John's Gospel and letters ("the Spirit of truth," "the light shines in the darkness," "the children of light," "the spirit of truth and the spirit of error," 1 John 4:6) has its closest Jewish parallel in the Two Spirits doctrine, so that the fourth Gospel, once thought Hellenistic, is now read as thoroughly Palestinian Jewish in its idiom. The community of property in Acts 2-4 ("all who believed were together and had all things in common... they sold their possessions and belongings and distributed the proceeds to all, as any had need") is the practice of 1QS, down to the sin of lying about property (Ananias and Sapphira's crime is the Rule's, with the penalty raised). The communal meal with bread and wine blessed by the priest, "the many" as a name for the assembly (Mark 14:24's "poured out for many"; Matthew 26:28), the twelve-and-three leadership council (twelve laymen and three priests, against the twelve and the inner three of the Gospels), the excommunication procedure of rebuke before witnesses and then before the assembly (Matthew 18:15-17 nearly exactly), and the self-designation "the Way" (Acts 9:2; 19:9) are shared furniture. And the Teacher of Righteousness, a persecuted founder whose reading of Scripture defined a community, has been compared to Jesus since the scrolls' discovery, and the volume states the comparison's honest limit: the Teacher is never called Messiah, never said to have died for anyone, never expected to rise, and his community awaited two Messiahs after him; the resemblance is of type (the persecuted teacher-founder), and the difference is the whole gospel.

The Hymns and the War

The Thanksgiving Hymns (1QH, the Hodayot) are some thirty psalms in the Psalter's manner, several apparently in the Teacher's own voice, and they supply the era's most personal religious poetry: the speaker's sense of himself as "a creature of clay, kneaded with water, a foundation of shame and a source of impurity," saved only by God's "abundant mercy" and "righteousness," his knowledge given by the Spirit ("I know you, my God, by the spirit which you have given me"), his persecution by "men of deceit," his community as a planting and a temple, and the theology of grace the volume flags for its resonance: "as for me, my justification belongs to God... by his righteousness he will cleanse me from the impurity of man and the sin of the sons of men" (the Community Rule's closing hymn, 11:12-15), justification by God's righteousness rather than the speaker's, in the language Paul would use of a different righteousness. The War Scroll (1QM) is a liturgy for the final battle, forty years of war between the sons of light and the sons of darkness (Belial's army, the Kittim, the Romans by the scroll's date), with trumpet-calls, banner-inscriptions, priestly exhortations, and the angelic hosts fighting alongside the community, Michael "exalted among the gods," ending in the destruction of darkness and the reign of light. Its interest for the New Testament is the pattern: Revelation's war in heaven and earth, Michael and his angels against the dragon, the armies of the Lamb, the two-age dualism, and the certainty that the end would come as a battle, all belong to the world the War Scroll inhabits, and the Gospels' Jesus, refusing the sword at his arrest ("do you think I cannot appeal to my Father, and he will at once send me more than twelve legions of angels?", Matthew 26:53, the War Scroll's angelic legions declined), is again the revision of an expectation the scrolls preserve.

The Commentaries: the Pesharim

The community read the prophets as written about itself, and its commentaries, the pesharim (from pesher, "interpretation," the word Daniel uses for the interpretation of dreams), run through Habakkuk, Nahum, Micah, Hosea, Isaiah, and the Psalms line by line, each verse followed by "its interpretation concerns..." and an application to the community's own history: the Chaldeans of Habakkuk 1 are the Kittim (Rome); the wicked who swallows the righteous is the Wicked Priest (a Hasmonean high priest) who persecuted the Teacher; the righteous who lives by his faith is "all those who observe the law in the house of Judah, whom God will deliver from the house of judgment because of their suffering and their faith in the Teacher of Righteousness" (1QpHab 8:1-3), Habakkuk 2:4 read, in the generation before Paul, as faith in a teacher, which is the datum the volume sets beside Volume Four's walk of the same verse: the community read "the righteous shall live by his faith" as loyalty to its founder's teaching; Paul read it as faith in Christ; both read it as the key to living through the interim. The method itself is the New Testament's: "this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel" (Acts 2:16) is a pesher formula, and the apostles' conviction that the prophets wrote of their own days and the Gospel's events is the same conviction the Qumran commentators held about theirs, the two communities sharing a hermeneutic and disagreeing about the referent. The Florilegium (4Q174) is a thematic anthology reading 2 Samuel 7 messianically ("I will be a father to him and he shall be my son: he is the Branch of David who shall arise with the Interpreter of the Law... in Zion at the end of days"), the datum Volume Two's panel on Nathan's oracle cited, and the Testimonia (4Q175) strings together Deuteronomy 18's prophet like Moses, Numbers 24's star from Jacob, and Deuteronomy 33's Levi blessing as the community's messianic proof-texts, the three offices, prophet, king, and priest, each expected.

The Two Messiahs, and the Three Texts That Sound Like the Gospels

The community expected two Messiahs: "the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel," a priestly anointed one who would preside and a royal anointed one who would fight, with a prophet like Moses before them (1QS 9:11; CD 12:23; 1QSa 2:11-22, where at the messianic banquet the Priest blesses the bread before the Messiah of Israel does), the offices the Hebrew Bible held apart kept apart in the sect's hope, which is the context for this series' repeated observation that Psalm 110 and Zechariah 6 fused them in one person and that the New Testament's Jesus is priest and king at once. And three fragments, small and famous, sound so much like the Gospels that they must be quoted. The "Son of God" text (4Q246, Aramaic, first century BC) describes a coming figure in the language of Daniel: "He will be called son of God, and they will call him son of the Most High... His kingdom will be an eternal kingdom, and all his ways in truth. He will judge the earth in truth and all will make peace," the titles "son of God" and "son of the Most High" and "eternal kingdom" appearing together in a Jewish text a century before Gabriel says to Mary, "He will be great and will be called the Son of the Most High... and of his kingdom there will be no end... the child to be born will be called holy, the Son of God" (Luke 1:32-35), the verbal correspondence so close that scholars debate whether the scroll's figure is the Messiah or an antichrist-like usurper claiming the titles, and either way the vocabulary of Luke's annunciation is shown to be Palestinian Jewish, not Hellenistic invention. The Melchizedek scroll (11Q13, first century BC) presents Melchizedek not as a Canaanite king but as a heavenly deliverer, an angelic figure who at the end of days proclaims the jubilee release, atones for the sons of light, judges the wicked with the holy ones, and is the "God" of Psalm 82:1 who "stands in the divine council," with Isaiah 52:7's herald of good news ("your God reigns") interpreted of him, the exalted Melchizedek of Hebrews 7 ("resembling the Son of God, he continues a priest forever") standing in a Jewish tradition of a heavenly Melchizedek already current, so that the letter's argument built on the one datum the Hebrew Bible supplies in two places. And the Messianic Apocalypse (4Q521, first century BC), the fragment the volume ranks as the most striking of all: "heaven and earth will listen to his Messiah... For the Lord will consider the pious, and call the righteous by name, and his spirit will hover over the poor, and he will renew the faithful with his strength. For he will honor the pious upon the throne of an eternal kingdom, releasing captives, giving sight to the blind, straightening the bent... and the Lord will perform marvelous acts such as have not existed, just as he said, for he will heal the wounded, and revive the dead, and bring good news to the poor," the list, drawn from Psalm 146 and Isaiah 61, being nearly verse for verse the message Jesus sent from Galilee to the imprisoned Baptist when John asked whether he was the coming one: "Go and tell John what you hear and see: the blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them" (Matthew 11:4-5; Luke 7:22), the two lists sharing their scriptural sources, their order, and their two rarest items, the dead revived and the poor evangelized, which appear together in no Old Testament text, so that a Jewish document buried before Jesus was born shows that "the dead are raised and the poor have good news preached to them" was a recognized description of the messianic age, and Jesus's answer to John was, in the vocabulary of at least one Jewish community, a claim to be that age's bringer.

Sealed in the New Testament the Dead Sea Scrolls are not cited in the New Testament, and no New Testament author is known to have read them; their relationship is that of a shared world, and the volume grades it so, with the three texts above standing at the top: Luke 1's annunciation titles (4Q246), Hebrews 7's heavenly Melchizedek (11Q13), and Matthew 11's answer to John (4Q521) each having a Jewish parallel a century old when the Gospels used the language. Below them: Isaiah 40:3 as the wilderness charter (1QS, the Baptist); the Two Spirits and John's dualism; the community of goods and Acts; Matthew 18's discipline procedure; the pesher method and "this is that"; Habakkuk 2:4 read as faith in a teacher; the two Messiahs against the one; and the War Scroll's angelic legions declined at Gethsemane. The lesson the library teaches is the one the volume has taught at every entry, sharpened: the New Testament's language was Jewish, its expectations were Jewish, and its surprises were surprises to Jews who expected, in detail, something else.

Why the scrolls are not on the shelf: the community's own compositions were sectarian by definition, the rules and hymns and commentaries of one movement, and the movement died in AD 68 with no successor to carry its books; the rabbis knew the Essenes only as a memory, and the church never knew the scrolls at all until 1947. They were never candidates. They are the era's time capsule, and this series has cited them at Nathan's oracle, at Isaiah 53's pierced Servant, at Psalm 22's "pierced," and at Habakkuk's watchpost, because a Jewish text from the generation before Jesus is the best possible witness to what a Jewish text meant in the generation of Jesus.

In plain terms: Nine hundred manuscripts from eleven caves, copied 250 BC to AD 68, the sealed time-capsule of one devout Jewish community (the Essenes) in the generation before Jesus: the Community Rule (the Two Spirits, property in common, the offering of the lips, Isaiah 40:3 as the wilderness charter), the Damascus Document (the Teacher of Righteousness and the Man of Lies), the Hymns (justification "by his righteousness"), the War Scroll (angelic legions at the last battle), and the pesharim (the prophets read of the community's own days, Habakkuk 2:4 as faith in the Teacher). Two Messiahs, of Aaron and Israel, with a prophet before them. And three fragments that sound like the Gospels: "son of God... son of the Most High... eternal kingdom" (Luke 1's titles a century early), a heavenly Melchizedek who atones and judges (Hebrews 7's tradition), and the Messianic Apocalypse's "revive the dead, and bring good news to the poor" (Jesus's answer to John, verse for verse). Never Scripture, never candidates; the best witness to what Jewish words meant when the Gospels used them.

The Witnesses

The Two Witnesses: Philo of Alexandria and Flavius Josephus

Philo: the Logos Waiting in Alexandria

Philo Judaeus (c. 20 BC to c. AD 50) was the leading Jew of Alexandria, the largest Jewish community outside Judea, a man of great wealth and greater learning, brother of the city's richest banker, uncle of the Tiberius Alexander who would later govern Judea and command Roman troops at the siege of Jerusalem, and, in the one public act of his life we can date, the head of the Jewish delegation to the emperor Caligula in AD 39-40 after the Alexandrian pogrom, described in his Embassy to Gaius. He was Jesus's older contemporary and never heard of him. His enormous surviving corpus, some forty treatises in Greek preserved entirely by the church (the synagogue lost him, as it lost the whole Greek-Jewish literature), is a sustained project: to read the Torah, in the Greek of the Septuagint, as the truest philosophy, and to show that Moses had taught, in the form of law and narrative, what Plato and the Stoics taught in the form of argument. His method is allegory: Abraham's migration is the soul's journey from sense to reason; Sarah is virtue and Hagar the preliminary studies; the four rivers of Eden are the four cardinal virtues; the high priest's robe is the cosmos; the literal sense is kept (Philo insists on keeping the laws literally as well as understanding them spiritually, and scolds the extremists who kept only the allegory) and the spiritual sense is the treasure. And his central doctrine, the one the church would find waiting for it, is the Logos: the Word, the Reason of God, the divine mind's pattern for the world and the agent of its creation, the "firstborn son" of God, the "image of God" through whom the world was made, the "high priest" who mediates between God and creation, the "second God" (in one daring passage), the "man of God" and "the beginning," the instrument by which the unknowable God is known, in Philo's system neither fully personal nor fully separate, a philosopher's bridge between transcendence and world.

Sealed in the New Testament no New Testament author quotes Philo, and the direct dependence once proposed for John's prologue is no longer widely held; the relationship is of a shared Hellenistic-Jewish vocabulary that the New Testament deploys with a different subject. John's "In the beginning was the Word (Logos), and the Word was with God, and the Word was God... All things were made through him... And the Word became flesh" (1:1-14) uses Philo's central term and three of Philo's predicates (the Logos as beginning, as with God, as the agent of creation) and then says what Philo could not have said, that the Word became flesh, a particular Jew in Galilee, the bridge of the philosophers arriving as a person; and the volume states the relationship precisely: John's vocabulary was in the air Philo breathed, John's claim was not. Hebrews' description of the Son as "the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature," through whom God "created the world" (1:2-3), Colossians' "image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation... all things were created through him" (1:15-16), and Hebrews' whole treatment of the heavenly sanctuary and the earthly copy, the high priest who enters the true tabernacle, use the Alexandrian idiom (image, firstborn, the heavenly pattern and its shadow) that Philo's treatises had made standard among Greek-speaking Jews, and Hebrews' allegory of Melchizedek ("without father or mother") is Philo's kind of exegesis of Genesis 14's silences. Beyond vocabulary, Philo is the New Testament's witness to what a Greek-speaking Jew of Paul's generation took for granted: the Septuagint as Scripture, the immortality of the soul, the Torah as the world's true philosophy, and the unknowable God knowable through his Word.

Why he is not on the shelf: Philo never claimed to write Scripture; he wrote philosophy about it. The rabbis never mention him and lost his Greek; the church preserved him so completely that Eusebius and Jerome listed him among Christian writers by adoption, and legend made him a convert, which he was not. He is the era's greatest Jewish thinker and its best witness to the world of the Greek Bible.

Josephus: the Priest Who Wrote the War

Joseph son of Matthias (AD 37 to c. 100), a Jerusalem priest of Hasmonean descent on his mother's side, a Pharisee by profession after trying all three parties, a commander of Galilee in the revolt against Rome, the survivor of the suicide pact at Jotapata, the prisoner who prophesied Vespasian's rise to the purple and was freed when it came true, and thereafter Flavius Josephus, the emperor's client in Rome, wrote four works in Greek that the church preserved and the synagogue forgot: The Jewish War (c. 75-79), the history of the revolt from the Maccabees to the fall of Masada, with the siege of Jerusalem described by a man who watched it from the Roman lines and pleaded with the defenders from the wall; the Jewish Antiquities (c. 93-94), twenty books retelling the whole biblical history from Genesis through the exile and continuing through the Persian, Greek, Hasmonean, Herodian, and procuratorial periods to the eve of the war, the indispensable source for the Herods, the high priests, Pilate, and the parties; his Life, a defense of his conduct in Galilee; and Against Apion, a defense of the Jews' antiquity against Greek slanders, which contains the passage on the twenty-two books the orientation page quoted. His importance for the New Testament is that he wrote the history the Gospels assume, from the other side: Herod the Great's paranoia and building program, the census under Quirinius, the succession of the tetrarchs, Pilate's provocations, the high priesthood's rotations (Annas and Caiaphas both appear), the Samaritan troubles, the famine under Claudius (Acts 11:28), the death of Agrippa I struck down at Caesarea in his silver robe (Acts 12, told by Josephus with an owl instead of an angel), the Egyptian prophet who led four thousand into the wilderness (Acts 21:38), the procurators Felix and Festus, and the war whose approach Jesus foretold and whose horrors Josephus narrated, the temple burning in August of 70, the daily sacrifice ceasing, the last defenders on Masada.

Sealed in the New Testament Josephus is not cited in the New Testament (he wrote after most of it), and the New Testament is not cited by him, but three passages of the Antiquities touch its people directly, and the volume states each at its scholarly weight. First, John the Baptist (18.116-119): Herod Antipas killed John, "who was a good man and had commanded the Jews to exercise virtue... and to come to baptism," because he feared John's influence over the crowds would lead to sedition, an account that confirms the Gospels' John and differs from them in motive (Josephus gives the political fear, the Gospels the Herodias affair; both may be true), and whose authenticity no one seriously disputes. Second, James (20.200): the high priest Ananus, in the interval between two procurators in AD 62, "convened the judges of the Sanhedrin and brought before them a man named James, the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, and certain others... and delivered them to be stoned," the death of the Lord's brother, the author of the letter this project's first series walked, recorded by a contemporary with the phrase "Jesus who was called Christ," authentic by the judgment of nearly all scholars and the earliest non-Christian reference to Jesus by name. Third, the Testimonium Flavianum (18.63-64), the paragraph on Jesus himself: "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if indeed one ought to call him a man. For he was one who performed surprising deeds and was a teacher of such people as accept the truth gladly. He won over many Jews and many of the Greeks. He was the Christ. And when, upon the accusation of the principal men among us, Pilate had condemned him to a cross, those who had first come to love him did not cease. He appeared to them spending a third day restored to life, for the prophets of God had foretold these things and a thousand other marvels about him. And the tribe of the Christians, so called after him, has still to this day not disappeared." The volume states the consensus honestly: the passage as it stands, in every Greek manuscript, contains confessions ("He was the Christ"; "if indeed one ought to call him a man"; the resurrection stated as fact) that a Pharisee who never became a Christian would not have written, and Origen, who read Josephus in the third century, said plainly that Josephus "did not believe in Jesus as the Christ"; but the passage's presence in all manuscripts, its Josephan vocabulary in its neutral clauses, its citation by Eusebius in the fourth century, and the survival of a tenth-century Arabic version that lacks the confessional phrases, have led most scholars to the middle position: an authentic notice by Josephus, that Jesus was a wise man and a wonder-worker, that Pilate crucified him on the accusation of the Jewish leaders, and that his followers persisted, into which a Christian copyist, probably in the third century, inserted the confessions. On that reading, the two most important facts a non-Christian historian of the first century could confirm, that Jesus lived and taught and was crucified under Pilate on the leaders' accusation, and that his brother James was stoned as "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ," are confirmed by the one Jewish historian whose works survive.

Why he is not on the shelf: Josephus wrote history, and said so; his Antiquities retell Scripture but do not claim to be it. The rabbis forgot him (the medieval Hebrew "Josippon" is a loose adaptation of the War, and knows nothing of the Testimonium); the church preserved him so faithfully, and read him so avidly for the fulfillment of Jesus's prophecies about Jerusalem, that his works were, after the Bible, the most copied history in Christian libraries for a thousand years. He is the era's chronicle, and the reader who wants to see the Gospels' world from the outside will find it nowhere else.

How the synagogue reads it

the synagogue lost both men. Philo's Greek allegories and Josephus's Greek histories belonged to the Hellenistic Judaism that the destruction of the temple and the rise of rabbinic Judaism left behind: the rabbis wrote in Hebrew and Aramaic, read the Torah through the Oral Torah rather than through Plato, and regarded Josephus, if they remembered him at all, as the general who surrendered. Neither is cited in the Talmud. Both were rediscovered by Jewish scholarship in the sixteenth century (Azariah dei Rossi read Philo in Latin and was astonished) and are read today by Jews as by Christians, as the two great witnesses of the century in which the temple fell and the two daughter faiths, rabbinic Judaism and Christianity, took their separate ways out of the ruins.

In plain terms: Philo (c. 20 BC to AD 50), Alexandria's philosopher-Jew, read the Torah as Plato's truth by allegory and taught the Logos, the Word of God, firstborn, image, agent of creation, mediator, the vocabulary John's prologue uses and then exceeds ("the Word became flesh": Philo's words, not Philo's claim), and Hebrews and Colossians speak his idiom of image, firstborn, and heavenly pattern. Josephus (AD 37 to c. 100), the priest-general who survived Jotapata and wrote the War and the Antiquities in Rome, is the Gospels' world from the outside: the Herods, Pilate, the high priests, the war, and three notices, John the Baptist killed by Antipas, James "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ" stoned in 62, and the Testimonium on Jesus, an authentic core (wise man, wonder-worker, crucified under Pilate on the leaders' accusation, followers persisting) with a Christian copyist's confessions inserted. Neither claimed to write Scripture; the synagogue lost both; the church kept both.

The Coda

The Companion Closed: What the Apostles Read, and What They Preached

Close the shelf and count what it held. Five books and several additions that the Greek Bible carried and half of Christendom reads as Scripture; a dozen visionary and testamentary works under the names of the ancients that no one canonizes and everyone in the first century read; the sealed library of a desert community; and the two great Greek-writing witnesses. Across all of them the volume's opening rule held at every entry: quotation is not canonization, and neither is echo, parallel, or shared vocabulary; the apostles read these books, thought in their categories, borrowed their phrases, cited two of them outright, and built on none of them, their Scriptures being the twenty-four the Lord had opened on the Emmaus road. And the shelf taught, cumulatively, the lesson the orientation page promised and the main series needed: the New Testament is a Jewish book, in Jewish words, answering Jewish expectations. Its Son of Man enthroned, its angels in chains, its Logos, its "son of the Most High," its sinless son of David, its heavenly Melchizedek, its "the dead are raised and the poor have good news preached to them," its sons of light, its community of goods, its "this is that," were all in the air of Judea and Alexandria before a word of it was written, and the reader who has walked this shelf can now hear, in the Gospels' every sentence, both the vocabulary the apostles inherited and the claim they made with it, which no book on this shelf had made: that the Messiah the Psalms of Solomon expected had come and been crucified, that the righteous man of Wisdom 2 had been mocked in those very words and vindicated on the third day, that the martyrs of Maccabees had been joined by a Martyr whose blood spoke a better word, that the two Messiahs of Qumran were one, that the Word Philo described had become flesh, and that the twenty-four books, read rightly, had said so all along. The Scriptures he opened were the twenty-four. The books between the Testaments are the room in which he opened them. Whoever is among you of all his people, may the LORD his God be with him. Let him go up.
