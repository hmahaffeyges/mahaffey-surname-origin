The Door

Why This Page Comes First

The Reader at the Door

If you have come to this series from "The Writers of the New Testament," you have watched the risen Jesus, on the road to Emmaus, open "the Scriptures" to two disciples and set their hearts on fire. The eight volumes ahead of you walk those Scriptures, all of them, in the order he named them. But before the first volume opens the Torah, one honest admission is owed: most Christians, including the ones who love the Old Testament, have never been told what a Jewish Bible actually is. What its shelves are, why its books stand in a different order from the Christian Old Testament, what the apostles' own copy looked like, which other Jewish books they read and quoted without ever calling them Scripture, and what a Jewish reader today studies alongside the text. Without that orientation, the series' whole method, walking Israel's Scriptures as Israel's, with the synagogue at the table, will feel like a novelty rather than what it is: reading the Old Testament the way the New Testament's authors read it. This page supplies the orientation, in seven parts, so that Volume One can begin where it should, in the beginning.

Part One

Part One: the Three Shelves

What "Tanakh" Means

The Jewish name for the Bible is an acronym. Tanakh spells the initials of its three divisions: Torah (the Teaching, the five books of Moses), Nevi'im (the Prophets), and Kethuvim (the Writings), T-N-K, Ta-Na-Kh. The name itself tells you the structure: not one long shelf of thirty-nine books but three shelves of unequal height and different kinds, gathered in that order, and read in that order of authority. The Torah is the foundation, the Prophets are its commentary and enforcement, and the Writings are the people's response and reflection, the whole arranged so that each shelf stands on the one before it.

The Torah, five books: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy. Israel's constitution, the story from creation to the border of the land and the law given at Sinai, the shelf every other shelf refers back to.

The Prophets divide in two. The Former Prophets are the books a Christian shelf calls history: Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings (Samuel and Kings each a single book in Hebrew), Israel's story from the conquest to the exile, told, the name insists, prophetically, as the record of the covenant kept and broken. The Latter Prophets are Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the Twelve, the minor prophets counted as one book on one scroll, from Hosea to Malachi.

The Writings are the widest shelf: Psalms, Proverbs, Job; the five festival scrolls (Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther); Daniel; Ezra-Nehemiah (one book); and Chronicles (one book), which stands last.

Count them the Hebrew way and you have twenty-four books; count them the Christian way, with Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, and Ezra-Nehemiah split and the Twelve separated, and you have the same text in thirty-nine. Nothing is added or removed between the two counts; the same words are simply bound differently. (Josephus, writing near the end of the first century, counted twenty-two, folding Ruth into Judges and Lamentations into Jeremiah, the same books once more.)

Jesus Named the Shelves

The tripartite Bible is not a later rabbinic invention, and the clearest evidence is on the Lord's own lips. On the evening of the resurrection he told the disciples, "everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled" (Luke 24:44), the three shelves named in order, with the Psalms standing for the Writings, as the first and largest book of that shelf naturally would. Earlier the same day, on the Emmaus road, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (24:27). And in his indictment of his generation he gave the shelf's outer edges: "the blood of all the prophets, shed from the foundation of the world... from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah" (Luke 11:50-51), Abel, the first murder in Genesis, and Zechariah, the priest stoned in the temple court in 2 Chronicles 24, the last recorded martyr in the last book of the Hebrew Bible, a sweep from the first book to the last that only makes sense on the Hebrew order. (The identification is debated, since Matthew's parallel calls him "Zechariah the son of Barachiah," the name of the prophet of the Twelve rather than the priest of Chronicles; the "between the altar and the sanctuary" of the same verse matches the Chronicler's temple-court scene, and the Abel-to-Zechariah sweep reads most naturally as Genesis-to-Chronicles. The series follows that reading and notes the question.)

The shelves are attested outside the Gospels as well. The grandson of Ben Sira, translating his grandfather's book into Greek around 132 BC, wrote a prologue referring three times to "the Law and the Prophets and the other books of our fathers," the three-part shape already in place a century and a half before Jesus. A Qumran document speaks of "the book of Moses, the books of the Prophets, and David." Philo of Alexandria described the Scriptures as "laws, and oracles delivered through the prophets, and psalms." And Josephus, defending the Jewish records against Greek critics, described a fixed collection, closed since the time of Artaxerxes, that "no one has ventured either to add, or to remove, or to alter" (Against Apion 1.38-42). The Bible Jesus read had three shelves, and everyone around him knew it.

In plain terms: Tanakh is an acronym for three shelves: Torah (the five books of Moses), Nevi'im (the Prophets, Former and Latter), and Kethuvim (the Writings, ending with Chronicles). Twenty-four books in Hebrew counting, the same words as the Christian thirty-nine. Jesus named the shelves in order ("the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms") and their outer edges ("from Abel to Zechariah," Genesis to Chronicles), and the three-part shape is attested from Ben Sira's grandson through Qumran, Philo, and Josephus.

Part Two

Part Two: What the Torah Is

The Foundation Shelf

Torah means teaching or instruction before it means law, and the word is used at three widths. Narrowly, the Torah is the five books of Moses, the Pentateuch, written on a single scroll and kept in the synagogue's ark, the most sacred object in Jewish life, carried in procession, stood for when raised, and kissed as it passes. More widely, Torah is the whole Tanakh, since all of it teaches. Most widely, in rabbinic usage, Torah includes the Oral Torah, the tradition of interpretation the rabbis held was given alongside the written text (Part Six returns to this). Volume One of this series walks the narrow Torah, the five books, and the reader should know why the five stand apart.

They stand apart, first, by origin: the Torah is the shelf attributed to Moses, the prophet "whom the LORD knew face to face," and every later prophet measures himself against it; the Prophets enforce it and the Writings meditate on it. Second, by use: the Torah alone is read through completely, every year, in the synagogue's weekly portions (the parashot, fifty-four of them), beginning again on the festival of Simchat Torah the day the cycle ends, so that a Jew who attends synagogue hears the whole of Genesis through Deuteronomy every twelve months, and the Prophets only in selected readings (the haftarot) chosen to accompany each portion. Third, by authority: for the halakhic tradition, the Torah's commandments (counted at 613 in the classic enumeration) are the binding core, and the rest of the Bible, however holy, does not add commandments to the list. When Jesus says "the Law and the Prophets," or when Paul says "the Law," they mean this shelf, in this position, with this weight.

The Torah's own story runs from the creation of the world to the death of Moses on the far side of the Jordan, ending, like the Tanakh as a whole, before the arrival: the land in view, not entered, the reader left at the border. Volume One's motto is the reason the whole series exists: "If you believed Moses, you would believe me; for he wrote of me" (John 5:46).

In plain terms: Torah means teaching, and at its narrowest it is the five books of Moses, written on one scroll, kept in the ark, and read through completely every year in fifty-four weekly portions. It is the foundation shelf: the Prophets enforce it, the Writings meditate on it, and its 613 commandments are the binding core of Jewish law. It ends at the border of the land, like the Tanakh itself, before the arrival.

Part Three

Part Three: the Jewish Bible in Jesus's Day and Today

The Same Twenty-Four

The first fact to fix is continuity: the Jewish Bible of the first century and the Jewish Bible printed today contain the same twenty-four books. There is no lost book of the Hebrew canon, no later addition, no book quietly dropped. What a Jew in Nazareth heard read in the synagogue and what a Jew in Brooklyn reads this Sabbath are the same texts, and the Dead Sea Scrolls, copied between roughly 250 BC and AD 70, confirmed that the Hebrew text itself was transmitted with astonishing fidelity across the thousand years between those scrolls and the medieval manuscripts on which printed Bibles are based. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran is, apart from spelling conventions and minor variants, the Isaiah in your Bible.

The Soft Edges

The second fact is honesty about the edges. The Torah and the Prophets were settled long before Jesus. The Writings, the widest shelf, had a few books whose status was discussed in the rabbinic academies in the decades after the temple's destruction. The rabbis' technical term for a canonical book was that it "defiles the hands," a ritual-purity rule that marked the sacred scrolls off from ordinary ones, and the Mishnah records that the status of Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs was debated (Rabbi Akiva's fierce defense of the Song, "the holy of holies," belongs to that debate); that Esther was questioned (it never names God, and the oldest Jewish library, at Qumran, has yielded no fragment of it); that Ezekiel's temple vision was reconciled with the Torah only by the labors of a scholar who burned "three hundred jars of oil" working through the difficulties; and that Proverbs' apparent self-contradictions (the back-to-back "answer a fool" sayings) required an explanation. The important point for the Christian reader is what these debates were: not votes on whether to admit new books, but discussions of books already received, already read, already in use, whose difficulties the scholars felt bound to resolve precisely because the books were Scripture. The old textbook claim that a "Council of Jamnia" closed the Hebrew canon around AD 90 is a modern construction that scholarship has largely abandoned: the academy at Yavneh discussed, it did not legislate, and the collection it discussed was the one Josephus had already described as closed.

The Wider Library

The third fact concerns the libraries around the canon. The Qumran community's caves yielded fragments of every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther, and alongside them copies of Enoch, Jubilees, Tobit, Ben Sira, and the community's own writings, a library much wider than the canon, held by a sect whose exact list of Scriptures we cannot reconstruct. The Samaritans held the Torah alone as Scripture, and still do. The Sadducees, the priestly party, were said by early Christian writers to accept only the Torah, though the evidence is contested (their denial of resurrection, which Jesus answered from Exodus rather than Daniel, suggests at least that they weighted the Torah decisively). Judaism in Jesus's day was not a single-canon religion with a single-canon debate; it was a Torah-centered religion with a settled Prophets shelf, a Writings shelf whose center was firm and whose edges were being tidied, and sects with wider or narrower libraries around it. The Pharisaic-rabbinic collection, the twenty-four, is the one that survived the temple's destruction, and it is the one Jesus's own citations match.

The Greek Bible

The fourth fact is the apostles' working text. Jews in Egypt had translated the Torah into Greek around 250 BC (the legend of seventy-two translators in the Letter of Aristeas gave the translation its name, the Septuagint, "the Seventy"), and the rest of the Scriptures followed over the next century and a half. By the first century the Greek Bible was the Scripture of the Jewish diaspora, and it became the Bible of the church: when Matthew quotes Isaiah 7:14 with parthenos, "virgin," he is quoting the Greek; when Hebrews argues from "a body you have prepared for me," it is quoting the Greek Psalm 40; most of the New Testament's Old Testament quotations follow the Septuagint's wording. Volumes Three and Five of this series stop at those places and show them. Two cautions belong here. The Septuagint's wording sometimes differs from the Hebrew, occasionally reflecting an older Hebrew text than the one the Masoretes preserved (Jeremiah is notably shorter in Greek; the Qumran scrolls show both editions existed side by side), and the series flags such cases where they matter. And the great Greek Bible manuscripts that survive, copied by Christians in the fourth and fifth centuries, contain additional books, Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ben Sira, Maccabees, and others, in varying selections, the codices' contents differing from one another. The old theory of a fixed, wider "Alexandrian canon" adopted by the church has not held up well: what the manuscripts show is a Greek-speaking Jewish and then Christian library whose edges were broader than the Hebrew twenty-four, without a single agreed list. Part Five and the companion volume return to those books.

The Bible Today

The fifth fact is the Hebrew text you can buy. Between the seventh and tenth centuries the Masoretes, families of scribes in Tiberias, fixed the consonantal text, added the vowel points and accents that preserve pronunciation and chant, and counted every letter, and their work survives in the Aleppo Codex (c. 930) and the Leningrad Codex (1008), the basis of every printed Hebrew Bible. A printed Tanakh today follows the Talmud's order of the Writings, with Chronicles last (the great medieval codices, interestingly, place Chronicles first among the Writings and Ezra-Nehemiah last, the order of the Writings being the one place where Jewish manuscripts vary, and the Talmudic order, ending on "let him go up," being the one printed editions and this series follow). Its Prophets are read on the Sabbath in the haftarah selections; its five scrolls are read on their festivals; its Psalms are prayed daily; and it is studied, as Part Six explains, with a commentary in the margin.

In plain terms: Same twenty-four books then and now, with the text's fidelity confirmed by the Dead Sea Scrolls. The Torah and Prophets were long settled; a few books of the Writings (Ecclesiastes, the Song, Esther, Ezekiel) were discussed after AD 70 as received books with difficulties, not as candidates, and "the Council of Jamnia" is a modern construction. Around the canon stood wider libraries (Qumran's) and narrower ones (the Samaritans'). The apostles' working text was the Greek Septuagint, whose wording the New Testament usually follows and whose manuscripts hold extra books in varying sets. The printed Hebrew Bible rests on the Masoretes' medieval codices and follows the Talmud's order, ending with Chronicles.

Part Four

Part Four: Why the Christian Old Testament Is Ordered Differently

Two Orders, One Content

Open a Christian Bible and the Old Testament runs Genesis to Malachi in four blocks: Law (Genesis-Deuteronomy), History (Joshua-Esther), Poetry and Wisdom (Job-Song of Songs), and Prophets (Isaiah-Malachi, the majors then the minors). This is not the Hebrew order; it is, in the main, the order of the Greek Bible, which grouped the books by genre and kind, moving Ruth next to Judges (same era), Chronicles next to Kings (same story), Lamentations next to Jeremiah (same author), and Daniel among the prophets (a visionary), and placing the Prophets last, so that the Old Testament ends on Malachi's promise of Elijah and the New Testament opens with John the Baptist in Elijah's clothing. The arrangement has a logic and a beauty of its own, and the church has read it for eighteen centuries. But it changes the last word (from "let him go up" to a warning of utter destruction), it hides the three shelves Jesus named, and it makes "from Abel to Zechariah" unintelligible.

The Protestant Old Testament contains exactly the Hebrew twenty-four (as thirty-nine) in the Greek order; the Roman Catholic and Orthodox Old Testaments contain those books plus the deuterocanonical ones (Part Five), also in the Greek order. Jerome, translating the Latin Vulgate around AD 400 directly from the Hebrew, kept the Greek arrangement but insisted on the Hebrew list, calling the additional books useful for edification but not for establishing doctrine, a distinction the Reformers revived and the Council of Trent (1546) rejected for the Catholic Church. None of this touches the text of the twenty-four, which every Christian communion and every synagogue shares.

Why This Series Follows the Hebrew Order

This series walks the Old Testament as the Tanakh, three shelves, Genesis to Chronicles, for three reasons. First, because Jesus cited it that way, and the series' whole method is to read the Scriptures as he opened them. Second, because the shelves teach: reading Joshua through Kings as the Former Prophets rather than as "history" changes how you read them (they are sermons in narrative form, the covenant enforced), and reading the Writings as a shelf shows the Psalter at its head answering the Torah, and Chronicles at its end retelling the whole story before the last word. Third, because the ending matters: a Bible that closes with "let him go up" is a Bible that ends unfinished on purpose, awaiting the one who went, and Volume Eight's closing shows why. The reader who knows both orders loses nothing and gains the Lord's own table of contents.

In plain terms: The Christian Old Testament follows the Greek Bible's genre-grouping (Law, History, Poetry, Prophets), ending on Malachi; the Hebrew Bible follows its three shelves, ending on Chronicles. Same twenty-four books in both (Catholic and Orthodox Bibles add the deuterocanon). This series follows the Hebrew order because Jesus cited it, because the shelves teach, and because the ending, "let him go up," is the one the Gospels answer.

Part Five

Part Five: the Other Books the Apostles Knew

A Wider Reading Than the Canon

The New Testament's authors read more than the twenty-four books, and they did not hide it. Jude quotes 1 Enoch by name and as prophecy ("Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied, saying, 'Behold, the Lord comes with ten thousands of his holy ones,'" Jude 14-15, which is 1 Enoch 1:9 nearly verbatim), and two verses earlier he refers to a dispute between the archangel Michael and the devil over the body of Moses that appears in no canonical book (early Christian writers traced it to a work called the Assumption, or Testament, of Moses). Hebrews 11, in its roll of the faithful, remembers those who "were tortured, refusing to accept release, so that they might rise again to a better life" and others "sawn in two" (11:35-37), the first a nearly certain reference to the Maccabean martyrs of 2 Maccabees 6-7 (the aged Eleazar and the mother with her seven sons), the second to the tradition of Isaiah's martyrdom under Manasseh preserved in a work called the Ascension of Isaiah. John's Gospel sets a scene at "the Feast of Dedication" in winter (John 10:22), Hanukkah, a festival founded not in the Torah but in 1 Maccabees 4, after the temple's rededication in 164 BC, Jesus walking Solomon's colonnade during a holiday the Hebrew Bible never mentions. Paul names Pharaoh's magicians "Jannes and Jambres" (2 Timothy 3:8), names that appear nowhere in Exodus but everywhere in Jewish tradition of the period. And beyond explicit citation, the New Testament's language is soaked in the books of the intertestamental centuries: Romans 1's indictment of idolatry walks the argument of Wisdom of Solomon 13-14 almost paragraph by paragraph; the mockers at the cross ("He trusts in God; let God deliver him now... for he said, 'I am the Son of God'") echo the taunt of the righteous man's persecutors in Wisdom 2; Jesus's "Come to me... take my yoke upon you... and you will find rest for your souls" stands close to Ben Sira's invitation to wisdom's yoke (Sirach 51:23-27); James's "be quick to hear, slow to speak" has a twin in Sirach 5:11; Tobit's "what you hate, do not do to anyone" (4:15) is the golden rule in its negative form a generation before the Sermon on the Mount stated it positively; and Daniel's "son of man" was developed, in the Similitudes of Enoch, into a preexistent heavenly judge in the very decades when the Gospels were written.

Why They Are Not on the Shelf

None of this made those books Scripture, and the reasons are worth stating plainly, because the Christian reader deserves to know why the synagogue drew the line where it did and why the church, on the whole, followed. The rabbinic principle, stated by Josephus and assumed everywhere, was prophetic authorship within the prophetic era: the Scriptures were written by prophets, and "from Artaxerxes to our own time the complete history has been written, but has not been deemed worthy of equal credit with the earlier records, because of the failure of the exact succession of the prophets." Books composed after the era of Ezra and Nehemiah, when, as 1 Maccabees itself laments, there was no prophet in Israel, could be valuable, true, even inspired in a loose sense, but they were not Scripture. Most of the books in question fail that test on their face: Ben Sira is dated by its author's grandson to the early second century BC; 1 and 2 Maccabees narrate events of the 160s BC; Wisdom of Solomon was written in Greek, in Alexandria, probably in the first century BC, under Solomon's name a millennium after Solomon; Enoch and Jubilees and the Testaments are pseudonymous, attributed to patriarchs who could not have written them. A second principle was language and provenance: the Hebrew canon was, with the exception of Daniel's and Ezra's Aramaic sections, a Hebrew collection, and books known only in Greek, or composed in Greek, stood outside it (the discovery of Hebrew Ben Sira at Qumran and Masada shows the principle was not mechanical, since the rabbis knew and quoted Ben Sira in Hebrew and still excluded it). A third was content and use: the sectarian works (Jubilees with its solar calendar, Enoch with its elaborate angelology) belonged to communities the rabbis did not represent; and the Mishnah warns against "the outside books" (sefarim hitzonim), a category the Talmud illustrates with Ben Sira, popular, quoted, and not Scripture. The rabbis called them, in effect, edifying reading that could be studied but not read liturgically as the word of God.

The church inherited both the books and the question. Greek-speaking Christians read them in their Greek Bibles alongside the canon, and cited them freely; Jerome, learning Hebrew in Bethlehem, drew the line where the synagogue drew it and named the extra books "ecclesiastical," read in church for instruction but not for establishing doctrine; Augustine argued for their inclusion; the Western church used them for a millennium without a formal ruling; the Reformers, returning to the Hebrew, printed them in a separate section titled Apocrypha or dropped them; and the Council of Trent defined them as canonical for the Roman Catholic Church, calling them deuterocanonical, "of the second canon," which is the word this series uses for the books Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include and Protestant Bibles do not: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Ben Sira (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch with the Letter of Jeremiah, 1 and 2 Maccabees, and additions to Esther and Daniel. The Orthodox churches receive a somewhat wider set. This series takes no side in that dispute beyond stating it fairly; it walks the twenty-four books every reader shares, and it gives the other books a companion volume of their own, because a reader who has walked the whole Bible deserves to meet the books the Bible's authors were reading.

In plain terms: The apostles read beyond the twenty-four and said so: Jude quotes Enoch as prophecy and refers to the Assumption of Moses, Hebrews 11 remembers the Maccabean martyrs and Isaiah's sawing, John sets a scene at Hanukkah (a Maccabean festival), Paul names Jannes and Jambres from tradition, and Wisdom, Sirach, and Tobit echo through Romans, the Sermon on the Mount, and James. The synagogue kept them off the shelf by principle: Scripture was prophetic writing from the prophetic era (these books are later, often pseudonymous, often Greek), and the rabbis filed them as "outside books," edifying but not the word of God. The church inherited the question, Jerome drew the synagogue's line, Trent drew a wider one, and the companion volume walks the books themselves.

Part Six

Part Six: What Jews Study Today

The Written Torah and the Oral Torah

A Christian who opens a Jewish study Bible for the first time is often startled by the margins: the biblical text sits in the center of the page surrounded by commentary, sometimes several commentaries in different typefaces, the page a conversation rather than a monologue. This is the Mikraot Gedolot, the "Great Scriptures," the traditional rabbinic Bible, and understanding it explains why this series' panels quote Rashi, the Targums, and the Talmud. Rabbinic Judaism holds that Moses received at Sinai not only the written Torah but an accompanying tradition of interpretation, the Oral Torah, transmitted teacher to disciple for centuries and finally written down when the destruction of the temple and the dispersion threatened its survival. The first great codification was the Mishnah (c. AD 200), compiled by Rabbi Judah the Prince, six orders of legal discussion covering agriculture, festivals, marriage, damages, sacrifices, and purity, terse and Hebrew. The rabbis of the following centuries discussed the Mishnah, and their discussions, the Gemara, were gathered around it into the Talmud, in two editions: the Jerusalem Talmud (c. 400) and the far larger Babylonian Talmud (c. 500-600), which became the central text of Jewish study, a vast Aramaic-and-Hebrew record of argument, law, story, ethics, and biblical interpretation, in which the passages this series cites (Sukkah 52a on the pierced Messiah and the son of Joseph; Sanhedrin 98 on the Messiah's coming) sit among thousands of others. Alongside the Talmud stands the Midrash, collections of rabbinic exposition of Scripture, legal (halakhic) and homiletic (aggadic), of which Genesis Rabbah, cited in Volume Six on Proverbs 8, is among the greatest. And the Targums, the Aramaic translations-with-paraphrase read in the synagogue when Hebrew ceased to be the vernacular, preserve interpretations older than the Talmud, which is why Volume Three cites the Targum of Isaiah calling the Servant "the Messiah."

The Commentators

The margins of the rabbinic Bible belong above all to Rashi, Rabbi Solomon ben Isaac of Troyes (1040-1105), whose commentary on the Torah and Talmud is the first thing a Jewish child learns to read with the text and the first thing this series' panels consult, because Rashi gathers the tradition's readings with unmatched economy and states, at the contested prophecies, exactly what the synagogue's reading is. Beside him stand Ibn Ezra (the grammarian), the Ramban (Nahmanides, the mystic and polemicist), the Radak (David Kimhi, whose Psalms commentary the panels use), and Maimonides, whose codification of the law and whose Thirteen Principles of Faith, including the coming of the Messiah, define orthodoxy to this day. When this series says "how the synagogue reads it," these are the readers it means, and it quotes them at strength because a Christian who has only heard the Christian reading of Isaiah 53 has not yet heard the argument.

The Life of the Text

Finally, the text is lived, not only studied. The Torah is read through annually in the weekly portion, with its haftarah from the Prophets; the five scrolls are read on their festivals (the Song at Passover, Ruth at Shavuot, Lamentations on the Ninth of Av, Ecclesiastes at Sukkot, Esther at Purim); the Psalms are prayed in the daily and Sabbath liturgy, the Hallel at the festivals, Psalm 145 three times a day; the Shema, from Deuteronomy, is recited morning and evening; and the Talmud is studied in the daf yomi cycle, a page a day, seven and a half years to the end, by tens of thousands worldwide. A reader who wants to understand why Volume Four called Hosea 6:6 the only verse Jesus twice assigned as homework, or why Volume Five called the Psalter the longest-running shared liturgy on earth, is understanding this: Israel's Scriptures are still Israel's, read aloud, argued over, sung, and kept, and the series' whole posture, the olive tree standing, the synagogue at the table, is nothing more than taking that seriously.

In plain terms: Rabbinic Judaism reads the written Torah with the Oral Torah: the Mishnah (c. 200), the Talmud built around it (Babylonian, c. 500-600), the Midrash (rabbinic exposition), and the Targums (Aramaic paraphrases older than the Talmud). The traditional Jewish Bible prints the text surrounded by commentators, above all Rashi (1040-1105), with Ibn Ezra, Nahmanides, Kimhi, and Maimonides beside him; these are the readers this series' panels quote. And the text is lived: the Torah read through yearly, the five scrolls on their festivals, the Psalms daily, the Talmud a page a day.

Part Seven

Part Seven: How to Read the Eight Volumes

The Series' Method

The series takes its charter from Paul's account of Israel's Scriptures read with and without the veil: "to this day, whenever Moses is read, a veil lies over their hearts. But when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed" (2 Corinthians 3:15-16). Its method is to read the Tanakh as the apostles read it after the resurrection: Israel's Scriptures, in Israel's order, walked book by book, with the Christian reading displayed at every summit and the synagogue's reading stated, at strength, beside it. Three conventions carry the method on every page:

The scarlet-and-gold badge, "Sealed in the New Testament," marks the places where an apostolic writer cites, alludes to, or builds an argument on the passage in hand, so that the reader sees the Old Testament's New Testament career as it happens.

The blue-bordered panel, "How the synagogue reads it," convenes at the contested texts (Isaiah 7:14, Isaiah 53, Psalm 110, Daniel 7, and their kin) and states the classical Jewish reading first and fairly, from Rashi, the Targums, and the Talmud, before the series answers, on the principle that a reading you have never heard at full strength is a reading you have not yet answered.

The gold box, "In plain terms," closes each section with the summary a reader can carry.

The Order of the Walk

Volume One, the Torah. Volume Two, the Former Prophets (Joshua through Kings). Volume Three, the Latter Prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel). Volume Four, the Twelve. Volume Five, the Psalms. Volume Six, the Wisdom Books (Job and Proverbs). Volume Seven, the Scrolls and the Court Tales (Ruth, Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel). Volume Eight, Chronicles, Ezra, and Nehemiah, closing on the Hebrew Bible's last word.
