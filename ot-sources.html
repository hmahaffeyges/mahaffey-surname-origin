The Old Testament Unveiled

Sources

The Hebrew text, the synagogue's readings, the archaeology, and the canon, with links

"Search the Scriptures, for in them... they bear witness of me."

The eight volumes of "The Old Testament Unveiled," the orientation page, and the companion volume keep their prose free of footnotes on purpose, as the New Testament series did. But every historical, textual, and rabbinic claim in them rests on public and checkable sources, and this page gathers the principal ones: the Hebrew text and its oldest manuscripts, the Greek Bible the apostles quoted, the Talmud, Targums, Midrash, and medieval commentators the series' "How the synagogue reads it" panels cite, the ancient witnesses to the canon, the inscriptions and artifacts named in the walk, the books between the Testaments, and the church's own rulings. Each entry says what the source is, what the series leans on it for, and where it can be read.

Two notes. First, the Jewish sources are linked to the two standard free Jewish libraries, Sefaria (the open digital library of Jewish texts, with the Talmud in the William Davidson English edition) and Chabad.org (the Tanakh with Rashi's commentary), so that every rabbinic reading the series states can be checked in the tradition's own editions rather than in a Christian summary of them. Second, Sefaria follows Hebrew verse numbering, which differs from English Bibles in a few places (Psalm 22:16 in English is 22:17 in Hebrew; Isaiah 9:6 is 9:5; Micah 5:2 is 5:1; Jeremiah 31:31 is 31:30, and the Psalms' superscriptions count as verses); links below go to chapters where the numbering differs, so the reader will find the verse a line away.

Supports: Volume Four's panel on the pierced one (the lament "for Messiah ben Joseph who was killed"); Volume Five's panel on Psalm 2 (God addressing "Ask of me" to the Messiah son of David).

The Talmud's longest discussion of the Messiah: his coming, his name, his sitting among the lepers at Rome's gate, and the reconciliation of "with the clouds of heaven" (Daniel 7:13) with "humble, riding on a donkey" (Zechariah 9:9).

Supports: Volume Three's Isaiah 53 panel (the leper scholar); Volume Seven's Daniel 7 panel (the cloud-or-donkey resolution); Volume Four on Zechariah 9.

Ancient witnesses to the canon and its order

The Prologue to Ben Sira (c. 132 BC)

The translator's preface referring three times to "the Law and the Prophets and the other books of our fathers."

Josephus, Against Apion 1.37-43

The first-century historian's description of a fixed collection of twenty-two books, closed since Artaxerxes, which no one "has ventured to add, or to remove, or to alter."

Supports: The orientation page on the canon in Jesus's day and the prophetic-era principle; the companion volume's rule for why later books were excluded.
