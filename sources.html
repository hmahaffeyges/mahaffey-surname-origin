The Writers of the New Testament

Sources

The witnesses behind the historical claims, with links

"Examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so."

The volumes of this series keep their prose free of footnotes on purpose: the aim is a readable walk through the books, not an academic apparatus. But the historical claims made along the way, about authorship traditions, martyrdoms, canon lists, manuscripts, and the ancient world, rest on real and checkable sources, and this page gathers the principal ones. Each entry says what the source is, what claims in the series lean on it, and where a public copy can be read. Where a claim in any volume seems to lack support here, that is exactly the kind of correction the footers invite.

A note on the links: they point to free, stable public editions (Early Christian Writings, New Advent, Project Gutenberg, Sefaria, the official manuscript projects) rather than paywalled academic editions, so that any reader can follow them; for the physical artifacts and a few modern reference points, encyclopedia articles are linked as the most durable public summaries, each carrying its own bibliography for those who want to go deeper.

The earliest Christian witnesses

Clement of Rome, First Letter to the Corinthians (c. 96)

A letter from the Roman church within one generation of Peter and Paul.

Supports: The martyrdoms of Peter and Paul under Nero; Paul reaching "the limits of the west" (the release after Acts 28); the earliest quotations of 1 Corinthians and Hebrews, placing Hebrews in Rome before the year 100.

Read 1 Clement at Early Christian Writings

Ignatius of Antioch, seven letters (c. 110)

Letters written on the road to martyrdom in Rome.

Supports: Early circulation and authority of the Gospels and Paul; the letter to the Ephesians praising their bishop Onesimus, behind the tradition discussed in the Philemon chapter.

Read Ignatius at Early Christian Writings

Polycarp of Smyrna, Letter to the Philippians (c. 110-140)

From a direct hearer of the apostle John.

Supports: Very early attestation of 1 Peter, the Pastorals, and a dozen other New Testament books, all woven into one short letter.

Read Polycarp at Early Christian Writings

Papias of Hierapolis, fragments (c. 95-120)

A collector of testimony from the last living links to the apostles, preserved in quotation by Eusebius.

Supports: Mark writing as Peter's interpreter, accurately but not in order; Matthew compiling the oracles; the whole "accent and interpreters" argument of Volumes Three, Four, and Six.

Read the Papias fragments at Early Christian Writings

Irenaeus of Lyons, Against Heresies (c. 180)

From the pupil of Polycarp, the pupil of John.

Supports: The fourfold Gospel as settled ("as there are four winds"); the traditional authorship of all four Gospels; John's Gospel from Ephesus in old age.

Read Against Heresies at Early Christian Writings

Clement of Alexandria and Origen (c. 190-250)

The Alexandrian teachers, largely preserved through Eusebius.

Supports: Mark's Gospel written at Rome from Peter's preaching; John as the "spiritual Gospel"; Origen's verdict on Hebrews ("who wrote the epistle, God alone knows"); ancient doubts about 2 Peter honestly recorded; the tradition of Peter's head-downward crucifixion.

Read Clement and Origen at Early Christian Writings

Tertullian of Carthage (c. 200)

The earliest major Latin theologian.

Supports: The Western attribution of Hebrews to Barnabas, cited as a known fact; early testimony to Paul's and Peter's martyrdoms at Rome.

Read Tertullian at Early Christian Writings

Serapion of Antioch (c. 200), via Eusebius

A bishop's ruling on a forged gospel found in use at Rhossus.

Supports: The canon capstone's exhibit of the tests working: "the writings that falsely bear their names we reject, as men of experience."

Read the account in Eusebius, Church History, Book 6

Eusebius of Caesarea, Church History (c. 325)

The first comprehensive history of the church, quoting sources now otherwise lost.

Supports: The antilegomena (disputed books) lists; the tradition of Paul's release and second imprisonment; Hegesippus on James the Just and on Jude's grandsons before Domitian; the "trophy" marking Peter's grave; the general framework of the canon's reception.

Read the Church History at New Advent

The Muratorian Fragment (c. 170-200)

The oldest surviving canon list, from Rome.

Supports: The working New Testament collection in the second century; the ruling on the Shepherd of Hermas ("it ought indeed to be read, but not publicly in the church"); Hebrews' Western hesitation.

Read the fragment at Early Christian Writings

Athanasius of Alexandria, 39th Festal Letter (367)

The Easter letter containing the first surviving list of exactly our twenty-seven books.

Supports: The canon capstone's endpoint: "in these alone is the teaching of godliness proclaimed."

Read Festal Letter 39 at New Advent

Jerome, Lives of Illustrious Men (c. 393)

Biographical notices of the early Christian writers.

Supports: The note that Jude's citation of Enoch caused its rejection by some, filed in Volume Six as an authenticity mark; various authorship traditions.

Read Lives of Illustrious Men at New Advent

Non-Christian ancient sources

Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews (c. 93-94)

The Jewish historian writing for a Roman audience, with no Christian interests to serve.

Supports: The martyrdom of "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, whose name was James" in 62, with the city's protest, the strongest external attestation in the series (Antiquities, Book 20); background on the priesthood, the temple, and the era throughout.

Read Whiston's Josephus at Project Gutenberg

Tacitus, Annals 15.44 (c. 116)

Rome's greatest historian on Nero's persecution.

Supports: The setting of 2 Timothy, 1 Peter, and Hebrews: Christians scapegoated for the fire of 64, "an immense multitude" convicted, Christ executed under Pontius Pilate.

Read the Annals at Wikisource

Pliny the Younger, Letters 10.96-97 (c. 112)

A Roman governor of Bithynia-Pontus asking the emperor Trajan how to prosecute Christians.

Supports: The legal atmosphere of 1 Peter in those exact provinces: suffering "as a Christian," for the name itself; early worship of Christ "as to a god."

Read the Pliny-Trajan correspondence at Early Christian Writings

Suetonius, Life of Claudius (c. 121)

Imperial biography recording the expulsion of Jews from Rome over disturbances "at the instigation of Chrestus."

Supports: The background of Acts 18: Priscilla and Aquila lately come from Italy; the dating spine of Paul's Corinthian ministry.

Read Suetonius at LacusCurtius

Jewish and other ancient texts

The Septuagint (3rd-2nd century BC)

The pre-Christian Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, the Bible of the apostles' Greek-speaking world.

Supports: The bridge arguments throughout: he'emin rendered by episteusen at Genesis 15:6; Joshua and Jesus as the same Greek name; the wording of most New Testament quotations.

Read the NETS translation of the Septuagint

1 Enoch (3rd-1st century BC)

The apocalyptic collection beloved at Qumran and in that era's Judaism.

Supports: Jude's quotation of Enoch 1:9, discussed with the quoting-is-not-canonizing frame in Volume Six.

Read 1 Enoch (Charles translation) at Sacred Texts

The Testament (Assumption) of Moses (1st century)

The Jewish work behind Jude's Michael-and-the-devil episode, surviving incompletely.

Supports: Jude verse 9 and the deference argument in Volume Six.

Overview and text information at Early Jewish Writings

Mishnah, Sotah 3:4 (compiled c. 200, preserving earlier rulings)

Rabbi Eliezer's dictum on teaching one's daughter Torah.

Supports: The before-photo in the argument on Paul's transformation regarding women, Volume Five's 1 Timothy 2 discussion.

Read Mishnah Sotah 3:4 at Sefaria

The pagan poets: Epimenides, Aratus, Menander

Greek literature quoted by Paul himself.

Supports: The Athens method: "Cretans are always liars" (Titus 1), "in him we live and move" (Acts 17), and the precedent for Jude's use of Enoch.

Paul's classical quotations surveyed at Early Christian Writings' background resources

Manuscripts and archaeology

Papyrus 52 (John Rylands fragment, c. 125)

The earliest generally accepted New Testament fragment: John 18, copied within living memory of the Gospel's publication.

Supports: The manuscript-gap argument of the canon capstone.

P52 overview (Wikipedia, with images and bibliography)

The Chester Beatty and Bodmer papyri (2nd-3rd century)

Substantial early codices of the Gospels and Paul's letters.

Supports: Paul's letters circulating as a collection; the early text of the Gospels; the abundance argument.

Browse manuscript images at the Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts

Codex Sinaiticus (c. 330-360)

The oldest complete New Testament, fully digitized by its holding libraries.

Supports: Complete Bibles older than the councils; the honest text notes on Mark's ending and other flagged passages.

Explore the full manuscript at the Codex Sinaiticus Project

Codex Vaticanus (c. 300-325)

The Vatican Library's great fourth-century Bible.

Supports: Same as above: the canon's content attested in vellum before Athanasius listed it.

View the digitized codex at the Vatican Library

The Temple Warning inscription (discovered 1871; second fragment 1935)

The actual Greek warning stones from Herod's temple barrier, threatening death to Gentiles who passed the soreg.

Supports: Ephesians 2's "dividing wall of hostility" as a physical object, Volume Five.

The inscription's history and text (Wikipedia)

The Gallio inscription at Delphi (c. 52)

A letter of the emperor Claudius naming Gallio as proconsul of Achaia.

Supports: The fixed point for dating Paul's Corinthian ministry (Acts 18) and, from it, 1 Thessalonians as the earliest Christian document.

The Delphi inscription explained (Wikipedia)

The Vatican necropolis and the "trophy" of Gaius

Excavations under St. Peter's Basilica uncovering the ancient cemetery and the second-century shrine that Gaius (quoted by Eusebius) called the trophy of Peter.

Supports: The burial tradition in Volume Six's biography of Peter.

The excavations surveyed (Wikipedia)

Later reference points

Martin Luther, Prefaces to the New Testament (1522)

The source of the "epistle of straw" remark on James, and of the Apollos conjecture on Hebrews.

Supports: Volume Seven's reception history, quoted with its context: Luther kept, translated, and preached James.

Luther's prefaces in English translation (various editions); overview at Wikipedia

Adolf von Harnack, "Probabilia" on Priscilla and Hebrews (1900)

The celebrated conjecture that Priscilla wrote Hebrews.

Supports: Volume Eight's candidate list, reported with respect and with the 11:32 masculine-participle complication noted.

The authorship question surveyed, with Harnack's proposal (Wikipedia)

Granville Sharp, Remarks on the Uses of the Definitive Article (1798)

The grammatical study behind "Granville Sharp's rule."

Supports: The deity texts at Titus 2:13 and 2 Peter 1:1: one article, two titles, one Person.

The rule stated and debated (Wikipedia)

Constitution of the USSR (1936), Article 12

"He who does not work shall not eat," adopted without attribution.

Supports: The strange afterlife of 2 Thessalonians 3:10, noted in Volume Five.

The 1936 constitution's text (Wikisource)
