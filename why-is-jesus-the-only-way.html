Eyewitnesses of His Majesty · A Study

Why Is Jesus the Only Way?

The hardest question, answered for the people it is hardest for

"I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved."

The question, stated at full strength

Here is the question as it is actually asked, by people who have thought about it and are not being difficult.

Jesus said, "I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me." Christians have taken that sentence and built a wall with it. On one side of the wall are the people who believe the right things about Jesus. On the other side is everyone else: the Hindu grandmother who fed every beggar who came to her door, the Buddhist monk who spent sixty years extinguishing his own selfishness, the Muslim doctor who prayed five times a day and treated the poor for nothing, the Jewish rabbi who kept the commandments Jesus kept, the atheist nurse who never heard a sermon she believed and never let a patient die alone. Most of the religions of the world honor Jesus. The Hindu will call him an avatar, the Buddhist a bodhisattva, the Muslim a prophet, the humanist a moral genius; nearly everyone agrees he is a person worth imitating. And the Christian's answer to all of them is: it is not enough. You lived the way he told people to live, and it does not count, because you did not sign the form.

That seems cruel. It seems arbitrary, since the form was available in Kansas and not in Kyoto. It seems small, since it makes the God of the universe into the keeper of a members' club. And it seems, to the person outside the wall, like the one thing about Christianity that a good person cannot in conscience accept, because accepting it would mean agreeing that the best people they have ever known are lost.

This study is written for that person. It will not soften the sentence Jesus said; it will read it. And it will try to show that the wall the objection describes was built by Christians, not by Christ, that the church's oldest teachers never held the view the objection attacks, and that the sentence, read where it was spoken and for what it claims, is not the cruelest thing in the Gospels but the widest.

Where the sentence was said

Start with the room.

It is the night before the crucifixion. Jesus is at supper with eleven frightened men who have just been told that he is leaving, that one of them will betray him, and that Peter will deny him before morning. He says, "Let not your hearts be troubled. In my Father's house are many rooms. If it were not so, would I have told you that I go to prepare a place for you? And you know the way to where I am going." Thomas, who is the one in every group who says what everyone is thinking, answers: "Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?" And Jesus says: "I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:1-6).

Notice three things about that scene, because the objection has forgotten all of them.

First, it is not a lecture about other religions. There are no Hindus in the room and none in view. It is an answer to a man who is about to lose his teacher and wants to know how he will find him again. The subject of the sentence is not who is excluded from heaven; it is how these eleven men are going to get to where he is going, which is through his death, which he is about to walk into first.

Second, "the way" is not a set of beliefs. Jesus does not say "I teach the way" or "I have found the way," which is what a teacher says; the Buddha said exactly that, and said it honestly. He says "I am the way," which is what no teacher says, because it makes the person the road. The claim is not that his doctrine is the only correct doctrine. The claim is that he himself, his life and his death and what is about to happen on the third day, is the means by which human beings reach God, in the way a bridge is the means by which people cross a river. That is a stranger claim than the one the objection attacks, and it has different consequences.

Third, the sentence is a promise before it is a restriction. Its first words are "let not your hearts be troubled," and its structure is: there is a house, it has room, I am going to make yours ready, and I am how you get there. The exclusion the objection hears, "no one comes except through me," is in the sentence, but it is there the way "no one crosses except by the bridge" is in a sentence about a bridge. It is a description of how the crossing works, spoken to people who are about to need one.

The other passages that say the same thing have the same shape. Peter tells the Sanhedrin, "there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved" (Acts 4:12), and he is talking about the man they crucified, standing before the men who crucified him, with a man they know was lame beside him, healed. Paul tells Timothy there is "one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all" (1 Timothy 2:5-6), and the sentence's second half is as important as its first: one mediator, and the one gave himself for all. Every "only" in the New Testament is attached to a "for everyone." That is not an accident of phrasing. It is the shape of the claim.

Three questions the objection has fused into one

The cruelty the objection feels comes from running three different questions together. Pull them apart and the picture changes.

Question one: Is Jesus the only means by which anyone is saved? Christianity answers yes. Whatever salvation is, it is accomplished by what he did, and by nothing else. This is the claim John 14:6 makes.

Question two: Must a person know Jesus's name, and believe the right things about him, to be saved by him? Christianity's oldest and largest traditions answer no, and have since the second century. This is the claim the objection thinks John 14:6 makes, and it does not; the next section is about it.

Question three: Are good people saved by being good? Christianity answers no, and here is the part the objection has not noticed: it answers no for Christians too. The Hindu grandmother is not being held to a standard the Christian is exempt from. Nobody is saved by being good, on the Christian account, including the Christian; the church's own saints are the first to say they did not earn anything. If goodness were the entrance requirement, the Buddhist monk would be inside and most of the church outside, and the church has always known it. "No one is good but God alone," Jesus said, to a devout man who had kept the commandments from his youth.

So the objection's picture, in which good people of other religions are shut out for lack of a password while worse people who know the password walk in, is not the Christian picture. It is a caricature that some Christians have believed and preached, and the objection is right to find it monstrous. The Christian picture is that no one walks in on his goodness, that everyone who walks in walks in on mercy, and that the mercy was purchased by one man for the whole world, whether or not the whole world has heard his name.

That third point is the hinge, and it needs to be shown, not asserted.

What the church has actually taught about the people who never heard

The objection assumes that Christianity teaches damnation for those who never heard of Christ. Here is what the sources say.

The Bible's own roll of the saved begins before Abraham and includes people who had no Scripture at all. Hebrews 11 lists Abel, Enoch, and Noah, and gives the rule that covered them: "whoever would draw near to God must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him" (11:6). Not a creed; a direction of the heart. Outside Israel the Bible keeps producing people who knew God without the covenant: Melchizedek, priest of God Most High in a Canaanite city, whom Abraham honored; Job, in the land of Uz, the Bible's own model of righteousness, with no Torah; Jethro, the Midianite priest who taught Moses; Rahab; Ruth; Naaman; the whole city of Nineveh, spared on one sermon.

Paul, who wrote "one mediator," also wrote that Gentiles who never had the law "show that the work of the law is written on their hearts, while their conscience also bears witness," and that God "will judge the secrets of men by Christ Jesus" on that basis (Romans 2:14-16): judgment by the light one had, and the judge is Christ. At Athens he told the philosophers that God made every nation "that they should seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward him and find him, yet he is actually not far from each one of us," and that "the times of ignorance God overlooked" (Acts 17:27-30). Peter, after the Roman centurion Cornelius, said, "Truly I understand that God shows no partiality, but in every nation anyone who fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him" (Acts 10:34-35). John's Gospel, the one that contains "no one comes to the Father except through me," also says that the Word "was the true light, which gives light to everyone" (1:9), and has Jesus say, "I have other sheep that are not of this fold" (10:16). And Paul explains how the cross could reach people who lived before it: Christ's death was set forth "to show God's righteousness, because in his divine forbearance he had passed over former sins" (Romans 3:25). The atonement is not dated. It reaches backward to Abel and, on the same logic, outward to the grandmother in Kyoto.

The most remarkable text is a parable of Jesus's own, and the objection should read it slowly. In Matthew 25 the nations are gathered before the Son of Man and divided, and the sheep are welcomed with the words, "I was hungry and you gave me food, I was thirsty and you gave me drink, I was a stranger and you welcomed me." And the sheep do not know what he is talking about. "Lord, when did we see you hungry and feed you?" They served him without knowing it was him. "As you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." The people welcomed in that parable are people who did not recognize Christ, and are told that they had been serving him all along. The same Gospel, two chapters earlier, records the reverse: "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the kingdom of heaven... on that day many will say to me, 'Lord, Lord, did we not prophesy in your name?'... And then will I declare to them, 'I never knew you'" (Matthew 7:21-23). Put the two together and the "password" theory of salvation is refuted by Jesus himself: people who never said the name are welcomed, and people who said it constantly are not known. What mattered was not the word but the heart's actual direction, which he could see and they could not.

The church read these texts and taught accordingly, from the beginning. Justin Martyr, around AD 150, wrote that Christ is the Word "of whom every race of men were partakers, and those who lived according to the Word are Christians, even though they were thought atheists, as among the Greeks Socrates and Heraclitus" (First Apology 46). Clement of Alexandria said philosophy had been given to the Greeks as the law was given to the Jews, "to bring them to Christ." Augustine held that the thing now called the Christian religion "existed among the ancients, and was not absent from the beginning of the human race." Aquinas, in the thirteenth century, taught that a person who had never heard the gospel could be saved by implicit faith, a trust in God's providence that would have embraced Christ had he been known, and gave the example of a man raised among wolves: if he followed the light of reason toward the good, God would send him what he needed, "by an angel if necessary." The Second Vatican Council put the church's settled teaching in a sentence: "Those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel of Christ or his Church, but who nevertheless seek God with a sincere heart, and, moved by grace, try in their actions to do his will as they know it through the dictates of their conscience, those too may achieve eternal salvation" (Lumen Gentium 16). The Orthodox tradition has taught the same in its own idiom for as long. And the Protestant who is suspicious of Rome can hear it from C. S. Lewis, who put it in one sentence in Mere Christianity: "We do know that no person can be saved except through Christ; we do not know that only those who know him can be saved through him."

Lewis went further, in a story, and the story is worth telling because it is the objection's own case answered by a Christian who believed John 14:6 completely. At the end of The Last Battle, a young soldier named Emeth, who has served the false god Tash all his life with a sincere and honorable heart, meets Aslan, whom he has been taught to hate, and expects to die. Instead Aslan says: "Son, thou art welcome... all the service thou hast done to Tash, I account as service done to me." Emeth objects that he has been Tash's servant, not Aslan's, and Aslan answers that no service that was not vile could be done to Tash, and no service that was vile could be done to him, and that Emeth had been seeking Aslan all along under the wrong name: "Unless thy desire had been for me thou wouldst not have sought so long and so truly. For all find what they truly seek." That is Matthew 25 as a story. It is also, almost exactly, what Justin Martyr said about Socrates eighteen centuries earlier. It is not a modern softening of the hard sentence; it is the oldest Christian reading of it.

So the direct answer to the question, "is the Buddhist who spent his life caring for others not going to heaven?", is this: not for being a Buddhist, and not for lacking a password. The Christian claim is that if he is saved, and the church hopes and prays that he is, he is saved by Jesus, who died for him whether or not he knew it, and who told a parable about people exactly like him being welcomed and surprised. The same door is open to him that is open to the Christian, and it is the same door, and the Christian did not earn it either.

Then why say "only" at all?

If that is the teaching, the objection will fairly ask, why not simply say that all religions are paths up the same mountain, and stop insulting people? The answer has four parts, and each is about what is true rather than about who is in.

First, because the claim follows from who Jesus said he was, not from a policy about membership. The Buddha said, in effect, "I have found the path; here it is; walk it." Muhammad said, "I am a messenger; here is the message." Confucius said, "Here is the way of the ancients; follow it." Every one of those is a claim about a teaching, and teachings can be compared, combined, and improved. Jesus said, "I am the way," and "before Abraham was, I am," and "the Son of Man came to give his life as a ransom for many," and "this is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins." He did not claim to have found something; he claimed to be something, and to be doing something on the cross that no one else could do. If that claim is false, he is not one path among many; he is a man who made a claim no sane teacher makes, and the courteous thing is to say so. If the claim is true, then "only" is not arrogance; it is arithmetic. If God became a man and died for the world, then that death is the means of the world's salvation, all of it, and there is no second such death to be the second way. A bridge built across a chasm is the only way across the chasm, not because the builder is jealous of other bridges, but because there is one bridge. The "only" describes the world, not the club.

Second, because the religions do not in fact describe the same mountain, and it is disrespectful to pretend they do. The picture of many paths to one summit is offered as humility, and it is the opposite: it tells every religion that it is wrong about its own destination. The Buddhist does not want the Christian heaven; the goal of the Buddhist path is nirvana, the extinguishing of the self and its desires, the end of rebirth, which is not a place and not a person and not communion with anyone. The Hindu traditions seek moksha, the soul's release from the wheel and, in the most influential school, its absorption into Brahman, in which the individual self is finally understood to have been an illusion. Islam's paradise is a garden awarded on a judgment of deeds, with no incarnation and no cross. Christianity's end is the resurrection of the body and the face-to-face knowledge of a personal God, in a renewed creation, forever, as oneself. Extinction, absorption, reward, and communion are not four names for one thing; they are four different things, and the traditions that teach them knew it and argued about it. To say "all paths lead to the same place" is to claim to see the summit that none of the climbers can see, which is a larger claim than any of theirs. The honest position is that the religions make different and incompatible claims about what is real, that they cannot all be right, that they could all be wrong, and that the question worth asking is which, if any, is true. That question can be investigated. This series' court-case handbook is an attempt to investigate it for one of them.

Third, because the goodness of the good Buddhist is not the thing in dispute, and Christianity has a name for it. The objection assumes that Christianity must deny or discount the goodness of people outside it. It does the opposite: it explains it. Every human being, on the Christian account, is made in the image of God, and every real goodness anywhere is that image showing through; the Hindu grandmother's mercy is God's mercy in her, the monk's discipline is God's order in him, and the Christian who cannot honor them has not understood his own doctrine. What Christianity claims is not that these people are not good but that goodness is not the problem to which Jesus is the answer. The problem, on every serious religion's account, is deeper than behavior: the Buddhist calls it dukkha and the craving that fuels it; the Hindu calls it the debt of karma and the wheel; the Christian calls it sin and death, the separation of the creature from the source of its life. These are different diagnoses, but they agree that something is wrong at the root that good deeds do not fix, since the deeds are done by the same self that is the problem. The Christian claim is that the root was dealt with, once, by God himself, at a cost, and that the good Buddhist needs that as much as the bad Christian does, and gets it the same way, which is to say by gift.

Fourth, because a loving God would want to be known as he is. If the Christian claim is true, then the deepest fact about the universe is that it was made by a person who loves what he made and came into it to get it back. A religion that did not say that, however good its ethics, would be leaving out the thing that matters most, the way a description of a marriage that omitted the spouse would be. The Christian who says "only way" is not saying "only us." He is saying that the God the Hindu grandmother was reaching toward has a face, and a name, and a wound, and that she is entitled to know it. Withholding the name in the interest of politeness would be the unkind thing. The gospel was carried to the ends of the earth by people who thought the nations deserved to hear who had died for them, and the nations, overwhelmingly, agreed: the largest churches on earth today are in Africa, Asia, and Latin America, among peoples who were not asked to abandon their goodness but told what it had been pointing at.

The objection turned around: a door is only cruel if the room was already yours

There is an assumption buried in the word "cruel," and it should be brought into the light, because once it is seen the whole question changes shape.

The objection pictures the default condition of a good human being as heaven, and Jesus as the gate that keeps some of them out. On that picture, "only way" is a restriction, and the God who imposes it is a jealous doorman. But that is not the picture any of the world's serious religions actually holds, including the ones the objection is defending. The Buddhist does not think the default is bliss; he thinks it is suffering and rebirth, endlessly, until something is done. The Hindu does not think the default is release; he thinks it is the wheel. The Muslim thinks it is judgment. The secular humanist thinks it is extinction. And the Christian thinks it is death and separation, which is what the story of Eden is about, and what every funeral confirms. No one thinks the room was already ours.

Change the picture accordingly. The default is not heaven but exile; the human race is on the far side of a chasm it did not dig and cannot cross; and the Christian claim is that one bridge was built, at a cost the builder paid, and that it is wide enough for everyone, and that the people walking across it in the dark, who never saw the sign with its name on it, are on it all the same. On that picture, "I am the way" is not a doorman's rule. It is the announcement that there is a way at all. A door is only cruel if it shuts you out of your own house. If you were outside in the cold and someone opened one, the only unkindness would be not telling you where it was.

This is why the New Testament's "only" statements always come with "everyone." "One mediator... who gave himself as a ransom for all." "God so loved the world." "I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself." "I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved." The door is singular; the "anyone" is the point.

What the Buddhist and the Hindu might say back

Fairness requires giving the other side its reply, and it has good ones.

The Buddhist may say: I do not want your heaven; I am not seeking a person to be reconciled with; the self you want to save is precisely the illusion I am trying to see through. That is an honest answer, and the Christian should respect it rather than pretend it is secretly Christian. The two traditions disagree about what a self is and whether it is worth saving, and the disagreement is real. What the Christian can say is that the Buddha diagnosed craving and suffering with a precision the church has always admired, and that Christianity's answer to the same diagnosis is not the extinction of the self but its death and resurrection, the self given away and returned. "Whoever loses his life for my sake will find it" is a sentence a Buddhist can hear. The difference is in what comes back.

The Hindu may say: I already honor Jesus; he is an avatar of the divine, one of many descents of God into the world, and your insistence that he is the only one is the provincialism of a people with one story. That too is honest, and it deserves the honest reply: an avatar in the Hindu sense is a divine appearance, and the Christian claim is that Jesus was not an appearance but a birth, a real human life that ended in a real death and a real resurrection in a datable year, outside a walled city, with witnesses, and that this is a different kind of claim from an avatar's, one that can be checked against history in a way the descents of Vishnu were never meant to be. The court-case handbook in this series is the check. If it fails, the Hindu is right to keep Jesus as an avatar and no more. If it holds, then something happened in Jerusalem that did not happen anywhere else, and the "only" follows from the event, not from the provincialism.

The Muslim may say: Jesus is a prophet, sinless and virgin-born, and we honor him more than you think; but God does not need a sacrifice to forgive, and no soul bears another's burden. The court case answers that at length in its seventh part, from the Qur'an's own ram at Eid. The short answer is that the Christian does not say God needed a sacrifice; he says God made one, of himself, because forgiveness always costs the forgiver, and that the one who paid was the one who was owed.

And the humanist may say: I do not need any of it; I will be good without a God. The Christian will answer, with the whole church behind him, that the humanist is very likely good, that the goodness is God's image in him whether or not he signs for it, and that the question is not whether he can be good without God but whether he can be right about the world without knowing what the world is. That is a question, not an accusation, and it is what this series exists to put on the table.

What this does not settle, and what it does

It does not settle the fate of any individual, and it must not pretend to. The judgment of persons belongs to God, who alone sees hearts; Paul's rule to the Roman church was "who are you to pass judgment on the servant of another? It is before his own master that he stands or falls" (Romans 14:4), and Jesus's was "judge not." The Christian who announces which of his neighbors is damned has taken a seat that was not offered to him. The church has canonized thousands of people and has never once declared, by name, that any person is in hell; it does not know, and it has said it does not know.

What this study does settle is the shape of the claim. "I am the way" is a claim about how salvation works, that it works through one person and what he did; it is not a claim that the people saved by him must have known his name, and the church's oldest teachers said so before the objection was raised. The Buddhist who spent his life in compassion, the Hindu grandmother, the Muslim doctor, the atheist nurse, are not shut out by a password they never heard. If they are welcomed, they are welcomed by Jesus, as the sheep in his own parable were, surprised, and told that they had been serving him all along; and the Christian standing beside them will be welcomed on exactly the same terms, which are mercy, and not a day of his own goodness.

"Only" was never about the size of the door. It was about who built it. And he said, in the same breath, that it was for anyone.
