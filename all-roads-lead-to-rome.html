Eyewitnesses of His Majesty · The Whole Story in One Image

All Roads Lead to Rome

Christianity, whole, for someone who was never told what it is

"I am the way, and the truth, and the life."

Correspondents of the Way · Heralds of His Word · Criers of Christ

A word about the line under the title. The apostles were the eyewitnesses. We are not, and we do not pretend to be. We are correspondents of the Way, reporting from a road we have walked; heralds of his word, running ahead with news of something that has happened; criers of Christ, standing at the crossroads calling out which way the road goes. This is not a challenge and not a recruitment. It is the good news, offered for free because it was offered to us for free: there is a road to God, it was built for you, and it is already yours if you want it.

The picture you were given

If you are outside Christianity, the picture you were given of it probably looks like this. It is a club. It has a doorman. Inside are people who believe the right things and behave the right way, and who look down on everyone who does not. Membership is earned by rules, kept by guilt, and enforced by the threat of a furnace. Its members are hypocrites, and worse: its churches have hidden predators, its history has crusades and inquisitions, and its God is a jealous old man who demands worship and punishes doubt. The kindest thing you can say about it is that some of its members are nice people despite it.

Almost every part of that picture is backward, and this study is an attempt to turn it around, using one image, all the way through. The image is an old one. Christians called their faith "the Way" before they called it anything else, and the word they used was the ordinary Greek word for a road. Jesus called himself the road. The oldest picture of the moral life in the Bible is two roads, one leading somewhere and one leading nowhere. And there is a proverb, older than the church, that says all roads lead to Rome, which was true for exactly one reason: Rome built them.

So here is Christianity as a road. Not a club; a road that was built before you were born, to a city you were made for, by the one who lives there, who came out along it to find you. Everything else is detail.

The map

Lay the pieces down first, so the rest can be read against them.

The CityThere is a destination. Call it Rome, because the proverb does; the Bible calls it the kingdom of God, the Father's house, the New Jerusalem, and, most simply, being with him. It is not a place you go when you die so much as a person you were made to be with, and a world remade around him. Everything in Christianity is about getting there and about who is there.
The WildernessEverything that is not the road. It is where the human race lives, east of Eden, and it is not neutral ground: it is dark, it has predators, and it goes nowhere. Every road in it that is not the road winds and ends. The Bible's word for being in it is "lost," and its word for its end is "death."
The RoadOne road runs through the wilderness to the City. It was surveyed long before it was lit, walked in the dark by a few, and then, at one point in history, the one who built it came out and walked it himself, all the way to the far end, through the one place no one could cross, so that it now runs, proven, from the last province to the gates.
The BuilderThe City's king built the road, from the City outward. He did not wait at the gates for travelers to arrive on their own strength. He came out. That is the single fact about Christianity that distinguishes it from every other account of God, and everything else follows from it.
The ChasmBetween the wilderness and the City there is a gap no traveler can cross by walking: death, and the separation from God that death is the sign of. The road had to be carried over it, and the Builder carried it at a cost that was his own.
The GuideTravelers on the road do not walk it by their own sense of direction. The Builder sends a guide, who gets in the car, who knows every mile, who says "this is the way, walk in it" at every fork, who recalculates without complaint after every wrong turn, and who never takes the wheel.
The AtlasThe road has a written record: the survey from before it was lit, the accounts of everyone who walked it, and the Builder's own words about where it goes. The record is not the road. It is the map of it, and travelers read it every day's march.
The CaravanPeople do not walk the road alone unless they insist. It was built for companies, with way-stations every day's journey where travelers eat at one table, rest, carry their wounded, and tell the newcomers what the next stretch is like.
The Destination FieldA road can be walked in either direction. What determines where a traveler arrives is not how far he has walked or how many times he has left the road and returned; it is the destination he has entered and keeps entered. The Bible calls this the heart. This study calls it direction of intent.

Now the story, in order, with the questions the outsider actually asks answered at each turn.

The question everyone asks first

Before the story, the objection, because it comes up the moment anyone says "one road." If Jesus is the only way, then are all the other religions simply wrong, and are the billions of people walking them headed to hell?

The problem is not with the answer. It is with the question. The question assumes that everyone is looking for the same destination, the same summit by different paths, and that assumption is the arrogant one, because it presumes that none of the travelers knows where he is going and that only the questioner can see the top. Christians are not claiming to be the only ones on the right path in a contest among climbers. They are saying something else: that the one who lives at the end of the road came out along it and showed it to us, because he made it for us and wants to live with us. Start with the proverb, because it answers the first half. All roads lead to Rome was true for one reason: Rome built the roads, from Rome, out to every frontier. It was never true of the world's roads. And when people say "all religions lead to the same God," they are saying something the religions themselves deny, because they are not trying to reach the same place. The Buddhist road is built toward nirvana, the extinguishing of the self and its desires, which is not a place and not a person. The Hindu road is built toward moksha, the soul's release from the wheel and, in its greatest school, its absorption into the One, where the individual self turns out to have been an illusion. The Muslim road is built toward a garden awarded on a judgment of deeds. The Christian road is built toward a person: the resurrection of the body and the face-to-face knowledge of the God who made you, as yourself, forever. Extinction, absorption, reward, and communion are not four names for one destination. Announcing that all the roads end in the same place does not honor the other travelers; it tells every one of them he does not know where he is going. It is like saying "all roads lead to Rome" to people who are not trying to get to Rome.

So when Jesus says "I am the way," he is not being a doorman. He is describing the one road to one particular city, and the reason there is one road is that the city has one builder, who built it from the inside out. "Only" was never about the size of the door. It was about who built it. And in the same breath he said the door was for anyone: "whoever comes to me I will never cast out."

That leaves the second half of the objection, the billions, and here the church's oldest teaching is not what the club picture told you. The road existed before it was lit, and people have walked it in the dark, by the intention of the heart, without knowing its name; Jesus told a parable in which the people welcomed into the city ask, "Lord, when did we see you hungry?" They had been serving him all along without knowing it. The church has taught since the second century that those who never heard are judged by the light they had, and saved, if they are saved, by the builder they never met. That is not a modern softening; it is Justin Martyr in the year 150. The good Buddhist is not shut out by a password he never heard. If he arrives, he arrives on the same road as the Christian, built by the same builder, and he will be as surprised as the sheep were.

What the gospel does for him is not lay a new road. It tells him what the road he is standing on is for, and turns the lights on. That is the rest of this study.

How we got into the wilderness

The Bible's first road is a closed one. When the man and the woman are sent out of the garden, God places "the cherubim and a flaming sword that turned every way to guard the way to the tree of life" (Genesis 3:24), and the Hebrew word is the ordinary word for a road. The first road in Scripture is the road back to life, and it is barred. The next thing the text records is Cain going "away from the presence of the LORD," to live "east of Eden," a wanderer (4:16). That is the wilderness by name: away from the presence, east of the garden, walking with no destination.

What opened the gate to the wilderness was not a rule broken for its own sake. The tree was called the knowledge of good and evil, and the serpent's offer was to have that knowledge by taking rather than by trusting: "you will be like God." The first humans chose to be their own destination, to enter their own name in the field, and the road to the tree closed behind them, not as spite but as consequence, because a creature that has made itself the center cannot stand in the presence of the real one. Everything the Bible calls sin afterward is that same choice in ten thousand forms: the self entered as the destination. And everything it calls death is where that road goes.

That is the outsider's first question answered: why is the world like this? Because it is the wilderness, and we are all born in it, east of the garden, on roads that wind. The Christian does not claim to be from somewhere better. He claims to have been told where the road is.

Why you already miss the City

Here is a fact about you that Christianity predicts and the wilderness cannot explain: you are homesick for a place you have never been.

Everyone is. The feeling of being lost, of belonging somewhere else, of a hunger that no food on earth quite satisfies, is universal, and every serious philosophy has had to account for it. Augustine's account is the first sentence of his autobiography: "You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you." Ecclesiastes says God "has put eternity into man's heart." Paul, speaking to Greek philosophers who had never heard of Moses, said God made every nation "that they should seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward him and find him, though he is actually not far from each one of us." C. S. Lewis put the logic plainly: if I find in myself a desire that nothing in this world can satisfy, the most probable explanation is that I was made for another world.

The Christian claim is that the homesickness is a memory of the garden and a compass toward the City, that you were made in the image of the one who lives there, for the purpose of knowing him, and that even before you know his name you are built to want to. The restlessness is not a defect. It is the destination field, empty, waiting for its entry, and reporting the absence.

That is the second question answered, the one the outsider rarely says aloud: why do I feel this way? Because you are a traveler who has not been told where the road is, standing in a wilderness that was never your home.

The road existed before it was lit

The road did not begin with Jesus. It was surveyed two thousand years earlier, and the survey crew was Israel.

God called one man out of the wilderness, Abraham, and told him to walk, and promised that through his family every family of the earth would be blessed. That family became a people, and to that people God gave the survey: the law, which is the guardrails on a road no one could yet see the end of; the prophets, who kept pointing down the road and describing the City; the psalms, which are the songs travelers sing; the temple, which was a model of the City built in the wilderness so people could see what they were walking toward; and the sacrifices, which taught, every morning, that the chasm ahead was real and that crossing it would cost a life. The Old Testament is the road atlas from before the road was lit, and the church has never stopped reading it, because you cannot understand where a road goes without the survey.

And people walked it in the dark. Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Ruth, David, the whole roll of Hebrews 11, who "did not receive what was promised" but "saw it and greeted it from afar," walked toward the City by intention, with no map of the far end and the chasm still uncrossed. Some arrived. It was possible, and it was not likely, and the Bible is honest that most of the road's early travelers wandered off it, the survey crew included.

That answers the question "what was the Old Testament for?" It was the road being built, mile by mile, through the history of one stubborn people, so that when the Builder came he came to a road that existed, with milestones already standing that said where he would appear, and what he would do, and what it would cost.

The Builder walks the road

Then, at a datable point, "when the fullness of time had come," the one who built the road came out along it in person.

That sentence is the whole difference between Christianity and every other road. In every other account of God, the traveler is expected to reach the City on his own legs, by keeping the rules, by discipline, by merit, by enlightenment, and God waits at the far end, and most do not arrive. In this account, the king of the City walked out to the last milestone, to the frontier where the people were who could not walk at all, and was born there, in a stable, to a poor family, in an occupied province, and walked the road as a traveler for thirty-three years. He did not survey the road from the palace. He walked it, in the dust, and got tired, and was hungry, and wept at a friend's grave, and was betrayed, and was tortured by the state. "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us." Immanuel: God with us. On the road.

And he walked it to the end, which is the chasm. Every traveler before him had stopped at death, because no one crosses it; the road ran to its edge and no farther. He walked into it. On a Friday outside Jerusalem, on a hill by a road, he was executed in the way the empire reserved for slaves and rebels, and the Bible says what that was: it was the Builder carrying the road across the gap at his own expense. "The wages of sin is death," and he paid them, not because a third party had to be punished but because forgiveness always costs the forgiver, and the one who was owed chose to absorb the debt rather than collect it. "God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them." The bridge over the chasm is made of his body. That is what the cross is. It is not a symbol of guilt. It is the one place on the road where the toll was paid for everyone who would ever walk it.

Then, on the third day, he came out the other side. The resurrection is the road proven: it goes through death, and the first traveler to make the whole journey stood in a garden on Sunday morning with the tomb open behind him, and said to a weeping woman, "Mary." From that morning the road ran, tested, from the wilderness to the City, and the announcement of that fact is what the word "gospel" means: news, the report a herald brings that something has happened: the road goes all the way, and it was built for you.

This is the answer to the question the outsider thinks is the hardest: why did Jesus have to die? Not to satisfy a bloodthirsty God, but because there was a chasm and no one else could carry a road across it, and the one who could, did, and it cost what it cost. And why does his resurrection matter? Because a road that stops at the cliff edge is not a road to the City; it's a road to the cliff. His coming back is the proof of where it goes.

The Guide gets in the car

The Builder did not walk the road and then leave the travelers to find their own way along it.

On the night before he died he promised his followers "another Helper," the Spirit of truth, who would "guide you into all the truth," and the word John uses is the word for leading someone along a road. Seven weeks after the resurrection the promise was kept, publicly, in Jerusalem, and the church was born. Isaiah had described the arrangement seven centuries earlier: "your ears shall hear a word behind you, saying, 'This is the way, walk in it,' when you turn to the right or when you turn to the left." Before that, Israel had been led through a literal wilderness by a pillar of cloud by day and fire by night, going ahead of a people who had never seen the road. The Guide is the pillar, with the light built in, and he gets in the car.

Understand what he does and does not do, because both matter. He knows every mile. He speaks at the forks. He recalculates after every wrong turn, without complaint, and says "make a U-turn when possible." He comforts in the valley. He reminds the traveler of everything the Builder said. And he never once takes the wheel. Direction of intent stays yours; the Guide does not drive. Christianity is not a religion of people who have handed over their will to a voice. It is a religion of people who have entered a destination and been given a companion who knows the way to it.

That is the answer to "why pray?" Prayer is not sending requisitions to a distant office. It is talking to the one in the passenger seat.

Entering the destination

Here is the center, and it is where the outsider's picture is most exactly wrong.

The club picture says: earn your way in. Be good enough, keep the rules, perform the works, and the doorman will let you pass. The road picture says: the road is already built, the toll is already paid, the Guide is already waiting, and the one thing required of you is a destination. Enter the City. Enter him. That is what the Bible means by "believe," a word that in Greek is pisteuō, to trust, and trusting is not an achievement; it is a facing, a direction, a choice about where you are going. "If you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved." No exam, no waiting period, no minimum mileage.

The proof that this is the actual teaching, and not a softening of it, is the last person Jesus spoke to before he died. A criminal, justly condemned by his own admission, dying beside him, with hours to live and nothing to offer, turned his head and said one sentence: "Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom." No baptism, no works, no creed, no time to walk anywhere. One entry in the field, toward the right person. And the answer came before sunset: "Today you will be with me in Paradise." Christianity is not about becoming good enough to be accepted. It is about learning that you will never be good enough, and that he wants to know you anyway, and that you were made for exactly that.

It is, so far as I know, the only religion in history in which the God comes to the traveler. Every other road expects the climb. This one's king built the road down the mountain, walked to the bottom, paid the toll, sent the Guide, and asks for a destination. "We love because he first loved us."

That answers the question "isn't it a religion of rules?" No. The rules were the guardrails on the unlit road, and they still mark its edges, but no one gets on the road by keeping them. You get on by turning toward the City. The rest is walking.

Walking it

But there is walking, and here the road picture keeps Christianity honest in the other direction.

Faith is a direction, and a direction is only real if you move. "Faith without works is dead," James wrote, not because works buy the road but because a traveler who has entered Rome and never moves has not, in fact, entered it. Paul says the same in one verse: "by grace you have been saved through faith... not a result of works, so that no one may boast," and then, "for we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them." Saved by grace; walking in works; the works prepared beforehand, like the road. The walking is not the ticket. It is what trust looks like from outside.

And the walking has a shape, and the shape is the beggar on the side of the road. Jesus told a story about a man beaten and left in the ditch on the Jericho road, passed by a priest and a Levite, and picked up by a Samaritan, a foreigner, who bandaged him, carried him, and paid for his care. The question the story answers is "who is my neighbor?", and the answer is: whoever is in the ditch beside the road you are on. Every traveler on the Way meets the beggar, and what he does is the test of whether he is walking toward the City or only saying he is. Jesus made it the criterion of the last judgment: "I was hungry and you gave me food, I was a stranger and you welcomed me." And the letter to the Hebrews adds the sentence that makes every traveler look twice at the man in the ditch: "Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for thereby some have entertained angels unawares." The beggar might be an angel. He is certainly Christ, who said so.

So the answer to "what does a Christian actually do?" is: walk toward the City, and feed whoever is in the ditch on the way. Everything else in Christian ethics is commentary on those two. There is one more thing the walking does, and it is the reason the road is taught the way it is. Most people will never read the four Gospels. What they will read is the fifth: the life of a Christian they know. "You yourselves are our letter of recommendation, written on our hearts, to be known and read by all," Paul told the Corinthians, "a letter from Christ... written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God" (2 Corinthians 3:2-3). Jesus's first instruction about the world was "let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works" (Matthew 5:16); see, not hear. Peter told wives with unbelieving husbands that the husbands "may be won without a word by the conduct of their wives" (1 Peter 3:1). And the Torah's own method for teaching the road to children is not a sermon: "talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way" (Deuteronomy 6:7). A child learns the road by watching where his father's feet go, and learns which forks matter by seeing which ones his father takes. The parent who lives it teaches it; the one who only preaches it teaches something else. "I have given you an example," the Builder said, with a towel in his hands, "that you also should do just as I have done to you."

The caravan

"Can't I be a Christian alone, without church?" You can walk alone. The road was built for caravans.

The Roman roads had way-stations, mansiones, one day's march apart, where travelers ate at a common table, slept in safety, and set out together in the morning. The church is that, and the way-station's furniture is the church's furniture. Baptism is the mark of having entered the road, the traveler's papers, given once. The Lord's Supper is the meal at the table, instituted by the Builder himself on the night before the chasm, with his own body and blood as the provisions, "do this in remembrance of me," and travelers have eaten it every day's march for two thousand years. The Lord's Day is the rest. The Bible is the atlas read aloud at the table so that the newcomer learns the road from the survey and the veterans are reminded of it. And the company is the point: the caravan carries its wounded, which is why the first thing the church built in the world was the hospital; it carries its children, who cannot walk yet; it corrects the traveler who has wandered; and the ones who have walked the next stretch tell the ones behind what it looks like. "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ."

That answers a question and raises the next one. The caravan is not a club; a club chooses its members. The caravan takes in anyone who says he wants to go to Rome, no questions, no credentials, no proof of character, because that is the Builder's own rule ("whoever comes to me I will never cast out"). And a company that open will always have people in it who are not going to Rome at all.

Robbers on the road

"Why are Christians such hypocrites? Why do predators hide in churches? What about the crusades and the inquisition?" These are the questions that keep the most decent people off the road, and the road picture answers them exactly.

A road that anyone may join will be walked by thieves, because thieves go where the travelers are. The predator hides among the sheep precisely because the sheep do not check credentials at the gate; the openness he exploits is the thing that makes it Christianity rather than a club, and the answer to him is not to close the gate but to do what the caravan has always been told to do, which is to watch for wolves and drive them out. Jesus said his followers would include them: "beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep's clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves"; wheat and tares would grow in one field till the harvest. He was betrayed by one of his own twelve. The church's crimes, the crusader who burned a city with a cross on his shield and the inquisitor who burned a man for his reading, were the acts of men who had the Builder's name in their mouths and a different destination in the field, their own power, their own kingdom. The road is not responsible for what people did with its name. And it is the road's own standard, "by their fruits you will know them," that every honest person uses to condemn them, which is worth noticing: the outsider judging the church's hypocrites is using the church's ruler.

As for the ordinary hypocrite, the Christian who is not much better than his neighbor: he is a traveler on a road, not a resident of the City. He is still in the wilderness's reach, he still wanders off, and the caravan is full of people at every stage of the journey, some of them a hundred yards past the gate. A hospital is full of sick people; that is not an argument against hospitals. The church never claimed to be a gathering of the good. It claimed to be a caravan of the lost who had been told where the road is.

Wrong turns

"What is sin, really? And what happens when I do it?"

Sin is leaving the road for the wilderness, in whatever direction, with the self entered as the destination. It is not a list of forbidden pleasures; it is the original choice of Eden repeated, to be one's own city. And the wilderness is real, which is why the Bible calls sin dangerous rather than merely naughty: the roads in it wind and end, and people are lost on them.

Repentance is the U-turn. The Greek word, metanoia, means a change of mind, a turning, and it is exactly what the voice asks for when it says "make a U-turn when possible." It is not groveling. It is re-entering the destination after an hour of driving the wrong way.

Forgiveness is recalculation without penalty. The system does not hold the wrong turns against the traveler, because the toll for every one of them was paid at the chasm, in advance, by the Builder. "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus." The Christian is not a person who stopped making wrong turns. He is a person who has learned that the voice will always recalculate, that the road is always where he left it, and that the father, in the story Jesus told about the son who left, was watching the road the whole time and ran.

What matters is not how many recalculations there were, or how long the trip took, or how far into the wilderness the traveler went before turning around. What matters is what stays entered as the destination.

Which raises the question a clever reader asks next, and Paul asked it first: "Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound? By no means!" (Romans 6:1-2). Can a traveler wander into the wilderness whenever he likes, since the voice will always recalculate? He can; and while he is out there playing, the wilderness is what he has entered as his destination, whatever his mouth says, and if the Builder comes down the road while he is gone, or if he gets lost out there and never finds the way back, he has risked everything for an afternoon. There is a difference between the traveler who stumbles off the road and turns around, and the traveler who lives in the wilderness and keeps Christ as a trail of breadcrumbs, a ribbon on a tree, to find his way back when it suits him. The second man has entered the wilderness as his destination and Jesus as his exit. He may never have entered the City at all. "No one who puts his hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God" (Luke 9:62); Lot's wife looked back at the city she had been pulled out of; the bridesmaids who were away buying oil when the bridegroom came found the door shut, and the lesson attached is "watch, for you know neither the day nor the hour." John draws the line exactly: "no one born of God makes a practice of sinning" (1 John 3:9), the practice, the settled direction, as against the stumble that every traveler makes and the voice forgives. Staying focused on the destination is what matters, not the occasional wander off the path; and only God can see what is entered in the field.

The valley

"If God is good, why do bad things happen to the people on his road?"

Because the road runs through the wilderness, not around it. The promise was never a road without a valley. The best-known psalm in the world says it: "Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil, for you are with me." Through the valley. The valley is on the road. The Jericho road, where the Samaritan found the beaten man, was a real road with real robbers, and the Bible never pretends that the Way is safe; its travelers were stoned, sawn in two, fed to lions, and the Builder himself died on it.

What the road offers in the valley is not exemption but company, the Guide in the car and the caravan around, and a destination that makes the valley a passage rather than a home. The Christian's answer to suffering is not an explanation. It is that the Builder walked into the worst of it first, that the cross stands in the deepest part of the valley, and that the road came out the other side on Sunday morning.

And the wilderness attacks the road because the road is a threat to it. The traveler who is walking toward the City will be tempted, opposed, and sometimes hurt precisely because he is on the road; the ones who are wandering are left alone. Jesus told his followers to expect it: "in the world you will have tribulation. But take heart; I have overcome the world."

The caravans disagree

"There are thousands of denominations. If God's road were clear, wouldn't there be one church?"

There is one road. There are many caravans on it, and they argue, and the arguments are almost always about how to travel and almost never about where the road goes. Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant Christians have disputed for a thousand years over the furniture of the way-station, how the meal is understood, who leads the company, how the atlas is read, and they have never disputed the destination: the same Nicene Creed, the same Builder, the same chasm crossed, the same City. The creed is the destination every caravan has entered. A traveler in any of them is on the road.

That is also the answer to "which church is the right one?" The one that is walking toward the City with the atlas open and the beggar fed. The rest is the caravan's business, and the caravans will settle it when they arrive.

The people in the dark, and the carried

"What about people who never heard? What about children, and those who cannot choose?"

The first was the subject of an earlier study in this series, and its answer is this: the road existed before it was lit, and people walked it in the dark by the intention of the heart. Jesus told a parable in which the people welcomed into the City say, "Lord, when did we see you hungry and feed you?" They had been serving him without knowing his name; they were on the road without the map. The church's oldest teachers, from the second century on, taught that such people are judged by the light they had and saved, if they are saved, by the Builder they never met. What the gospel does for them is not lay a new road. It tells them what the road they are on is for, and turns the lights on, so that they can walk it in daylight instead of the dark and stay on it when the wilderness looks inviting.

The second is simpler. Every caravan carries its children. The ones who cannot walk yet, and the ones who will never be able to, are carried by the company and by the Builder, who said "let the little children come to me, and do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of heaven," and who was himself carried, as an infant, into Egypt, on a road.

The end of both roads

"Is hell real? And what is heaven?"

The road picture answers both at once, and it is the subject of the study that precedes this one. Death is the moment when direction becomes destination. Nobody ends up somewhere he was not heading. The traveler who entered the City and walked toward it, however badly, arrives there; the one who entered himself and walked into the wilderness arrives where the wilderness goes, which is nowhere, in the dark, outside a lit house where a feast is going on. The Bible's own definition of hell is a privation, "away from the presence of the Lord," and its images, fire and darkness, are two pictures of the one absence. It is not a furnace God stokes. It is the end of the other road, and the door, as Lewis said, is locked from the inside.

And heaven is Rome: not a cloud but a city, a remade earth with the City come down onto it, the tree of life standing in it whose road was guarded with a sword in the third chapter of Genesis and is open in the last chapter of Revelation, and the King in the middle, who is the reason for all of it. "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people, and God himself will be with them." The destination was never a place. It was him.

And the Builder is coming back along his own road. The Christian hope is not that we go up but that he comes down, the City descending to the road's end, the wilderness reclaimed, every valley lifted, every tear wiped, death itself thrown away. The road is not permanent. It is for the meantime. When he comes, there will be no more wilderness to run it through.

Where the analogy breaks

Every picture breaks, and this one breaks in four places, and the four places are the gospel.

The road is a person. "I am the way." You do not walk on a surface; you walk in a relationship, and the relationship is with someone who is alive and knows your name.

The Guide is a person. Not a voice in a box but the Spirit of God, who loves you, grieves when you wander, and is described in the Bible as a comforter, an advocate, and a fire.

The City is a person. "That where I am you may be also." Rome is not a place with a king in it; it is the King, and the place is wherever he is, and the reason you were homesick was never for streets.

And the Builder died on his own road. No engineer dies to carry a road across a gap; he sends laborers. This one came himself, as a laborer, as the poorest traveler on the road, and the chasm was crossed by his death, and the toll was his blood, and he did it for the travelers, most of whom did not know his name and some of whom were the ones killing him. "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us." No analogy carries that. It is the thing the analogy was for.

How to enter it now

If you have read this far, you are standing at the fork, which is where everyone stands. The road is where it always was. The toll is paid. The Guide is waiting. The caravan will take you as you are, without credentials, and will not check your character at the gate, because the Builder told them not to.

What is required is a destination. Not a performance, not a rulebook, not a period of self-improvement first; those come with the walking, and the walking comes after. What is required is to turn toward him, and mean it, and say so. The thief said it in a sentence, and it was enough: "Jesus, remember me." Paul wrote it in a verse: "if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved." And the King himself said what happens when the destination is entered: "whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life. He does not come into judgment, but has passed from death to life."

Has passed. Present tense. The moment the destination is in the field, you are on the road, and the one who built it has said in as many words that he will not lose you on the way.

You were not made for the wilderness. You were made to know him, and he came out to find you, and the road is under your feet.
