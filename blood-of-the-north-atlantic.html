The Blood of the North Atlantic

A Letter to My Three Children on Ólavsøka

To my children:

What follows is the story of your dad’s ancestors. My half of you, and where it came from.

Daniel, Michael. I think you will find this part interesting. Your little sister shares the same ancestral heritage the Faroese do. The men who settled these islands came out of the Norse colonies in Ireland and the Isles, and the women they brought with them were Gaels. Norse Gaels, both halves of them. That is exactly what our family is: Bains and McDonalds out of Argyll and the Hebrides, Mahaffeys out of Galloway. Alexis is not visiting your heritage. She has it.

Luke, Ashlynn, Liam. The same goes for the three of you here at home, and one day you will stand on that ground yourselves.

This is the saga of your bloodline. Read it, copy it, and carry it with absolute pride.

The Cradle of Scotland: The Kingdom of Dál Riata

This half of the story is my mother’s. Her roots lie in Argyll (Earra-Ghàidheal, meaning “The Coast of the Gaels”), and so, through me, do yours. Around 500 AD, Gaelic-speaking Celts crossed the Irish Sea to settle this breathtaking landscape of sea-lochs and mist-shrouded islands. They founded Dál Riata, a sophisticated maritime empire.

Its nerve center was Dunadd Fort, a towering dark-age citadel built atop a volcanic crag in the Moine Mhór (“Great Moss”). It was here, at Dunadd, that early Scottish kings were inaugurated. They would place their bare foot into a footprint carved directly into the living bedrock, swearing a sacred oath to the land itself. Your ancestors were the foundational Gaels who brought the Gaelic language, deep monastic literacy, and the legendary Stone of Destiny to Scotland.

Somerled and the Fearsome Gallowglass

In the 9th century, Viking longships breached the horizon. While early contact was bloody, it rapidly evolved into deep cultural fusion. The Norse and the Gaels intermarried, creating a highly resilient, bilingual population known as the Gall-Ghaidheil (“Foreign Gaels”).

From this hybrid culture arose the Gallowglass (Gallóglaigh), an elite class of heavily armored mercenary warriors who wielded massive, two-handed Danish axes. They were the most feared shock-troopers in the British Isles, famous for their code of honor: they were forbidden to retreat and would either win the field or die where they stood.

The name tells you what they were. Gallóglaigh breaks into gall, the word for a foreigner and specifically for a Norseman, and óglach, a young warrior. Foreign warriors. The foreign half was the Norse half, and they came out of the Hebrides and the western seaboard, from precisely the families this letter is about.

They are first recorded in 1259. Aedh Ó Conchobair, King of Connacht, married a daughter of Dubhghall mac Ruaidhrí, lord of the Hebrides, and her dowry was one hundred and sixty mailed warriors under the command of her uncle. The Annals of Connacht and the Annals of Loch Cé both record it.

And here is the part worth stopping on. You can date this whole business off our own genealogy. Somerled died in 1164. His son was Raghnall. Raghnall had two sons: Domhnall, who gave Clan Donald its name, and Ruaidhrí. Ruaidhrí’s son was Dubhghall. Which means the man who sent the first gallowglass into Ireland was the nephew of the man our clan is named for, and he did it ninety-five years after Somerled went in the ground. Four generations, and there is a date on every one of them.

Push it back further and the arithmetic still holds. Norse ships struck Iona, in Somerled’s own country, in 795, and again in 802, and again in 806. Within two generations the Irish annals had a name for what the mixing was producing: Gall-Ghaidheil. By 1098 the Isles were formally Norwegian ground. Somerled himself carries a Norse name, Sumarliði, summer traveller, which is what a Norseman called a man who went raiding in the warm months, and he married Ragnhild, daughter of Olaf, the Norse King of Man. His great-grandson Dubhghall fought for Norway at Largs in 1263, and in 1266 the Treaty of Perth handed the Isles to Scotland, which is exactly when the Hebridean fighting families needed a new employer and Ireland became the answer.

Four hundred and sixty-four years from the first longship off Iona to the first gallowglass contract, and every step of it sits inside our own family’s records. That is why I can give you the dates with a straight face.

The trade ran for three and a half centuries. Irish lords granted gallowglass families hereditary estates in exchange for permanent service, so they arrived with wives and children and never went home: the MacSweeneys in Donegal, the MacDonnells in Antrim, the MacRorys, the MacCabes, the MacSheehys. They were organised in companies of about a hundred called a corrughadh. By 1512 there were fifty-nine of those companies in Ireland. In 1569 Turlough Luineach O’Neill married Agnes Campbell and took a dowry of roughly twelve hundred of them. Shakespeare knew the word well enough to put it in Macbeth.

It ended at Kinsale in 1601. Firearms and an English victory finished the age of the axe, and though Highland mercenaries kept crossing over into the 1640s, the gallowglass proper were done after three hundred and forty-two years.

Before Somerled there was a Norwegian king who wanted Kintyre and got it by cheating.

In 1098 Magnus Barelegs made a treaty with Edgar of Scotland: he could keep every island he was able to sail around with his rudder set in place. Magnus looked at the map, went to Tarbert where the neck of land between the sea lochs is barely a mile across, and had a skiff hauled overland from one shore to the other with himself sitting at the helm the whole way. By the letter of the agreement he had sailed around Kintyre. It was therefore an island. It was therefore his.

That king was the grandson of Harald Hardrada, and Harald Hardrada was the half-brother of Olaf Haraldsson, the King of Norway who fell at Stiklestad in 1030 and became Saint Olaf.

Read that again, and think about where you were standing. Ólavsøka is Olaf’s wake. The Faroe Islands shut down for three days every July for a man whose own kin, two generations on, dragged a boat across dry land to take the peninsula Margaret Templeton would be born on. Alexis, you were at his festival in one country, and you come from the ground his family took in the other.

Kintyre stayed Norwegian for fifty-eight years. Then it met Somerled.

In 1156, a legendary Norse-Gaelic chieftain named Somerled led a brilliant naval uprising, shattering the power of the King of Norway at the Battle of the Epiphany. Somerled crowned himself King of the Isles, ruling a sovereign maritime empire.

And then he married into the other side. Somerled’s wife was Ragnhild, daughter of Óláfr, King of Man and the Isles, and the Chronicle of Man names their four sons. So the house that became Clan Donald was founded by a Norse-Gael carrying a Gaelic name and the daughter of a Norse king, and that king was called Olaf. In Gaelic, Amhlaibh, which is exactly where the surname MacAulay comes from: son of Olaf.

There is your festival again, standing one generation away from the founder of your mother’s clan.

His direct descendants became Clan Donald (The MacDonalds). When King Robert the Bruce made his desperate bid for Scottish independence in 1314, Angus Og MacDonald brought a massive fleet of Highland galleys (birlinns) to his aid. At the Battle of Bannockburn, the MacDonalds formed Bruce’s elite reserve force, crushing the English army and securing Scotland’s freedom.

The Fair-Haired Bains of Clan Chattan

As the MacDonalds ruled the western waves, the MacBains (McBeans) emerged as a fierce member of the Clan Chattan Confederation, a historic alpine alliance of Highland clans whose crest featured a wild cat with the motto “Touch not the cat bot a glove” (Touch not the cat without a glove).

Clan Chattan is not Argyll. It is the eastern Highlands: Badenoch, Strathnairn, the country running south from Inverness. And the MacBeans are a clan of it in their own right, not a name hanging off somebody else’s. Their seat was Kinchyle, in the parish of Dores on the south shore of Loch Ness, with cadet houses at Faillie in Strathnairn and Tomatin in Strathdearn. Their own motto is a shade harder than the Mackintosh version you usually see: Touch not a catt bot a targe. Not without a glove. Without a shield.

And there are receipts. MacBean of Kinchyle signs the Clan Chattan Bond of Union in 1609. He signs the Bond of Maintenance in 1664. He signs the Letter of Authority to Mackintosh in 1756. Three documents across a century and a half, every one of them carrying a MacBean chief’s name as a principal of the confederation.

How they arrived there is the only genuinely open question. One tradition brings them out of Lochaber in the retinue of Eva, heiress of Clan Chattan, and settles them in east Inverness-shire. Another makes them a Moray family akin to the MacBeths, kin of the royal line that Malcolm II drove from the throne, who went looking for shelter among the descendants of Gillechattan Mór. The earliest hard record of the name is a Kinrara manuscript of the mid fourteenth century, naming Bean Macmilmhor and his son Milmor MacBean.

They paid for their loyalty the way Highland clans did. Heavy losses at Harlaw in 1411, fighting under Mackintosh. Transportation to the plantations of Virginia and the Carolinas after the rising of 1715. And at Culloden, Gillies MacBean, a man said to stand six feet four, set his back into a breach in a stone wall and cut down thirteen men, Lord Kerr among them, before they finished him.

Hold on to that last one, because Robert Bain was eight years old when it happened.

In Gaelic, Bàn meant “fair-haired” or “white.” When Highland families migrated into the Lowland trading networks, English-speaking clerks dropped the “Mac” prefix, transforming the name into Bain. Your Bain ancestors were proud highlanders who carried the genetic markers of these historic mountain warriors.

The Transatlantic Journey: Campbeltown to the Carolinas

On August 7, 1698, your ancestor Margaret Templeton was born in Campbeltown, a bustling deep-water port on the southern tip of the Kintyre Peninsula. Built over an ancient 6th-century monastic cell founded by Saint Kieran, Campbeltown was surrounded by the historic lands of the MacDonalds. Margaret later moved to Edinburgh, marrying Daniel Bain on December 21, 1721.

Their son, Robert Bain, was born in Stirlingshire in 1738, right on the geographic fault line between the Highlands and Lowlands. When Robert was just 8 years old, the Highland clan system was brutally shattered at the Battle of Culloden (1746). The British government banned the Gaelic language, outlawed tartan, and dismantled clan authority.

Refusing to live under occupation, a 17-year-old Robert Bain boarded a merchant ship in 1755 and braved the perilous Atlantic alone. He made his way to North Carolina, settling in the Cape Fear River valley, the largest Gaelic-speaking enclave in the New World.

That was not a random destination, and he was not the first of ours to think of it.

Sixteen years earlier, on the sixth of June 1739, the customs house at Campbeltown recorded the ship Thistle of Saltcoats, Robert Brown master, taking aboard emigrants for Cape Fair in America. She sailed that July with about three hundred and fifty people aboard under Neil McNeill, and what she carried was not passengers but a community: McNeills and McAllisters off the Argyll coast, families out of Kintyre and Gigha and Islay and Jura. That is the Argyll Colony, and for a time it was the largest settlement of Highlanders anywhere outside Scotland. Its leaders wrote to the Presbytery of Inveraray asking for a minister who could preach in Gaelic, because, as they put it, from that country all our servants are to be, many of which cannot speak any other language.

Note the port. Campbeltown. The town Margaret Templeton was born in.

Then came Culloden, and the trickle became a torrent. Robert sailed in 1755 into a valley that was by then full of people from his mother’s country, speaking his mother’s language. And when he married in 1760 he married a McDonald, out of the clan that had ruled the ground Campbeltown stands on.

So when I say he braved the Atlantic alone, I mean the crossing. He did not arrive alone. He arrived among his own.

There, in 1760, he married 16-year-old Martha McDonald, who had been born in Scotland in 1744. By marrying a McDonald, Robert reunited our family lineage with the very Hebridean sea-kings who had ruled Kintyre centuries prior. Together, this young, iron-willed couple survived the American Revolution, cleared the primitive wilderness, raised 10 children, and laid the foundations of the American Piedmont. Robert died in Cabarrus County on the twentieth of April, 1801, and Martha followed him four years after. They lie side by side in the churchyard of Philadelphia Presbyterian at Mint Hill, in Mecklenburg County. You can go and find them. They are not a story, they are a place you can drive to.

The Royal Bloodline of Ireland: Niall of the Nine Hostages

This half is my father’s, and it comes with something you can actually test for. My Y-DNA carries a world-class genetic signature: the R1b-M222 haplogroup. This is the literal biological barcode of Niall of the Nine Hostages, the legendary 5th-century High King of Ireland who ruled from the sacred Hill of Tara. Read the note at the end of this letter about that last sentence before you repeat it to anyone, because I want you carrying this accurately.

It does not reach the six of you the same way, and I want to be exact about it. Daniel, Michael, Luke, Liam, you carry it. The Y chromosome passes from father to son and to nobody else, so the M222 in me is the M222 in you, handed down man to man in an unbroken chain out of Ireland into Galloway and across an ocean, nothing added, nothing lost, no other line mixed in. It is the single most conservative thing any of you owns.

Alexis, Ashlynn, you do not carry it, because daughters do not receive a Y chromosome at all. Not from me, not from anyone. What I want you to understand is that this changes nothing about where you come from. You descend from every last one of those men exactly as your brothers do, through the same fathers, in the same order, without a single gap. You are simply not carrying their chromosome around as proof. The men are still yours. The castle is still yours. Craigcaffie does not check.

Niall was a brilliant warlord whose descendants, the Uí Néill, maintained an unbroken monopoly on the High Kingship of Ireland for nearly six centuries. During the early Middle Ages, elite warrior lineages from Niall’s dynasty crossed the Irish Sea, bringing their royal genetic markers into the rugged, untamed peninsula of southwestern Scotland known as Galloway.

The Sovereign Lords of Galloway and Kellechaffe Estate

Galloway was so culturally distinct and heavily fortified that it operated as an independent Celtic-Norse principality, entirely separate from the rest of Scotland. Its grand patriarch was Fergus, Lord of Galloway (d. 1161), a master diplomat and warrior who built a sprawling castle at Kirkcudbright and married the daughter of King Henry I of England. Fergus’s descendants were so powerful that they issued their own laws, commanded their own navies, and intermarried directly into the Scottish Royal line.

The Fortress of Craigcaffie and the Birth of McHaffie

Neil of Carrick was appointed Regent of Scotland in 1255. His daughter, Marjorie, was a fierce, independent Countess who famously abducted the man she loved to force a marriage, and their eldest son became the legendary King Robert the Bruce.

Neil’s son, John, was the maternal uncle to the Liberator King. In the early 1320s, following his historic victory over the English, King Robert the Bruce rewarded his loyal cousin John, “son of Neil,” by granting him a magnificent, sprawling estate just northeast of Stranraer in Wigtownshire: The Lands of Kellechaffe.

John’s descendants took the surname Neilson (meaning “sons of Neil”) and became a dominant force in Wigtownshire. Around 1570, the Neilsons constructed Craigcaffie Tower (an evolution of the name Kellechaffe) upon those royal lands. Craigcaffie is a spectacular, unyielding Scottish tower house. Built from massive dressed stone, it features defensive gun loops, crow-stepped gables, and projecting machicolations designed to rain projectiles down on attackers. It survived centuries of clan warfare and stands completely intact to this very day.

In the local southwestern Scots-Gaelic dialect, the tenant farmers, cadet branches, and sworn defenders of this estate took their name from the land itself. The spelling shifted phonetically across generations. In 1416, ecclesiastical documents show Thomas Makillehochufy serving as a high-ranking church prior at Whithorn (the ancient Casa Candida founded by St. Ninian). By the 1600s, parish registers list the name interchangeably as McIlhaffie, McHaffie, and eventually, Mahaffey. You are a Neilson and a Galloway Gael by blood, tied directly to the ground beneath Craigcaffie Castle.

Outlaws for Conscience: The Killing Times

Your ancestor John McHaffie lived at Gargerie, a remote farmstead in the rugged Kirkcowan parish of Wigtownshire. In the late 17th century, the Stuart kings attempted to force the Church of Scotland to accept royal supremacy. Southwestern Scotland revolted. Thousands of Scots signed a sacred covenant, declaring that only Jesus Christ, not the King, could head the church. They became known as the Covenanters.

The Crown responded with absolute military terror during a period known as “The Killing Times” (1680–1688). Royal dragoons hunted Covenanters through the Galloway moors. If an individual refused to swear an oath abjuring the Covenant, they were shot dead on the spot, their bodies left in the heather.

John McHaffie was a defiant leader in this underground religious resistance. In October 1684, John was captured during a massive military sweep of Wigtownshire. Rather than being executed and risk creating another martyr, the Privy Council ordered him permanently banished to the American plantations.

Chained in the dark hold of a merchant ship, John crossed the Atlantic as a political exile, landing in Virginia. His descendants inherited his unyielding grit. They pushed westward into the Appalachian frontier, fought for American independence, and formalized the modern spelling of MaHaffey by the American Civil War.

Why It Had to Be Bruce

You may have been told that Robert the Bruce gave those lands to his mother’s brother. That is exactly right, and it is worth saying plainly, because a great many websites get it wrong.

Bruce’s mother was Marjorie, Countess of Carrick in her own right, and she was the daughter and heiress of Niall, Earl of Carrick. So John, son of Neil, was Marjorie’s brother and the King’s own uncle on his mother’s side. When Bruce signed Kellechaffe over to him in the 1320s, he was not rewarding a distant retainer. He was providing for his mother’s family.

And here is why that matters far beyond one estate. Bruce’s father’s people were not Scottish at all. The de Brus family came out of Normandy with the Conqueror and held Annandale and lands in Yorkshire. Everything Gaelic in Robert the Bruce came down through his mother. From her he inherited the Gaelic earldom of Carrick, and by all accounts he grew up with three tongues in his mouth: Gaelic first and natively, Anglo-Norman French among the nobility, and the early Scots of the burghs. In the Gaelic of his own century he was Roibert a Briuis.

That is the thing almost nobody else in his position had. The other claimants to the throne were Norman lords who ruled Gaels. Bruce was a Norman lord who was also one of them, who could speak to the west in its own language, and whose first refuge when everything collapsed in 1306 was not a castle but the Gaelic country his mother came from: Carrick, Galloway, Argyll, the Isles. He was hidden, fed, and rebuilt by his mother’s people. Bannockburn was won eight years later by an army that existed because the Gaelic west had taken him in.

Now, about the Niall kings. I have to be careful with you here, because there are two men named Niall in this story and they lived eight hundred years apart. Niall, Earl of Carrick, your ancestor and Bruce’s grandfather, was a thirteenth-century Scottish lord whose family descended from Fergus of Galloway. Niall of the Nine Hostages was a fifth-century Irish high king. The name was common. A documented descent from one to the other has never been shown, and I would not want you repeating it as fact.

But do not be disappointed, because the true connection is better than the invented one.

In 1315 Bruce sent his brother Edward into Ireland with an army, and Edward was proclaimed High King. The man who made that possible was Domnall Ua Néill, king of Tír Eoghain, the direct heir of the Cenél nEógain and of the very Uí Néill dynasty founded by Niall of the Nine Hostages. Domnall stood down his own claim to the High Kingship of Ireland and handed it to a Bruce. And in about 1317 he put his name to the Remonstrance of the Irish Princes, a letter to Pope John XXII arguing that the Irish and the Scots were one people, sprung from a common origin and sharing a language and a way of life, and that they should be one kingdom under one crown.

So the House of Niall did come into our story, and it was not by a bloodline nobody can prove. It was by a king of the Uí Néill looking at the son of a Gaelic countess of Carrick and deciding that here, at last, was someone the Gaelic world could stand behind. That project failed at Faughart in 1318 and Edward Bruce was killed. But for three years there was a serious attempt to make Ireland and Alba a single Gaelic kingdom, and it happened because one Norman king had one Gaelic mother.

Every stone in Craigcaffie sits on ground that was handed over inside that story.

And there is one more thing about Bruce that I only assembled while writing this out, and it is the part I am proudest of.

He is on every side of us.

On my mother’s side, the MacDonalds: Angus Óg brought his galleys and his Islesmen to Bruce at Bannockburn in 1314, held the flank, and Clan Donald’s grip on Kintyre was the reward. On my mother’s side again, the MacBeans: a Myles MacBean is recorded fighting for Bruce against the Red Comyn, which is the quarrel the entire war grew out of.

And on my father’s side we are not his soldiers at all. We are his family. His mother was a Carrick. His uncle was John, son of Neil. And the ground our surname came out of, Kellechaffe, is ground the King put into that uncle’s hands.

So one man stands at the join of all three of my lines. Two of them fought for him. The third was him, near enough. And the estate that eventually turned into the name you carry was a gift from his own hand to his mother’s brother.

That is not a coincidence I arranged. That is what happens when a family stays in one corner of the world long enough for the same king to matter to all of it.

The Ancient Confluence

To understand why the three of you standing together in Tórshavn today is so poetic, we must erase modern political borders and look at the North Atlantic through the eyes of a 10th-century Viking mariner.

When Norwegian Vikings sailed west around 800 AD, they did not find an empty ocean. They found a vibrant, deeply sophisticated Celtic world. The epicenter of their new territories was the Hebrides, the shores of Argyll, and the deep bays of Wigtownshire, the exact territorial homelands of your ancestors.

For nearly three hundred years, our paternal and maternal lines lived in a massive cultural and genetic melting pot with the Scandinavians. They shared ship-building technology, blended their art styles, and fought side-by-side in shield walls.

When Norse chieftains decided to push further into the unknown north to colonize the remote Faroe Islands, they populated their longships using the communities they had established in Western Scotland. They traveled with Gaelic wives, Gaelic blood-brothers, and Gaelic companions.

Modern population genetics has proven this beyond any doubt. When scientists analyzed the DNA of the modern Faroese people, they discovered an incredible asymmetry: while the paternal Y-chromosome DNA is predominantly Scandinavian Norse, the maternal mitochondrial DNA is over 80% Celtic Gaelic, originating directly from Scotland and Ireland.

Who Actually Settled These Islands

Daniel, Michael, you will have grown up with the short version: Norwegians came, found empty islands, and stayed. That is not quite what happened, and the difference is the whole reason I am writing this.

Start with the first man named. Færeyinga saga opens with a single sentence: Maðr er nefndr Grímr kamban; hann byggði fyrstr manna Færeyjar. There was a man named Grímur Kamban; he was the first to settle the Faroes. Grímur is Norse. Kamban is not. It is Gaelic, from camb, crooked, and it is the sort of byname a man picks up living among Gaelic speakers. So the first settler of these islands carried a Norse given name and a Gaelic nickname, which means he almost certainly came not from Norway but from the Norse colonies to the south, out of Viking Ireland or the Isles. Tradition puts his farm at Funningur, on Eysturoy.

That is the pattern for everything after him. The people who filled these islands were not sailing straight out of the fjords. A great many of them staged through Ireland, the Hebrides, Orkney and Man, where Norsemen had already been living among Gaels for generations, marrying them, raising bilingual children and turning into something new. Gall-Ghaidheil. Norse Gaels. The identical fusion I described to your sister back in Part One, out of the same water.

And they did not sail with empty ships. They brought households, and those households were Gaelic on the women’s side. That is the entire explanation for the genetics. The Faroese carry Norse paternal lines and Gaelic maternal lines in one population because the men who came were Norse or half Norse, and the women who came with them were Gaels out of Ireland and western Scotland. Iceland is the same story with the same numbers for the same reason.

Which brings it back to the six of you. When I came to these islands in the nineties, that was not the first time our family’s blood arrived here. It was the second. Norse Gaels carried it ashore in the ninth century and it has been in the population ever since. A thousand years later I carried it ashore again. Daniel, Michael, you have it from both directions at once. And Alexis has the same second arrival you do.

A Footnote About the Monks

One more thing, for the curious, and it is only a footnote. I want to be careful with it because I had it slightly wrong myself the first time I wrote this out.

In 2013, archaeologists at Á Sondum on Sandoy dug beneath the floor of a Viking longhouse and found charred barley in burnt peat, carbon dated three to five hundred years before the Norse. Barley does not grow wild here. Somebody carried it across open ocean and planted it. An Irish monk named Dicuil, writing around 825, described northern islands where hermits out of Ireland had lived about a century until Norse raiders drove them off. Their name is still on the map where you are standing: Paparøkur near Vestmanna, Papurshílsur near Saksun. Papar, the fathers.

So Gaels did get here first. But they are not where the Gaelic in the Faroese came from, and I do not want you repeating that they are. They were monks. Celibate men. Whatever else they did here, they had no children, and they left or were driven out before the ships came.

The blood came later, and it came married.

Which Side Were We On

One of you is going to reach Culloden in this letter and ask the obvious thing. Would we have been out there, and on whose side?

We would have been out there twice, facing each other.

Take my mother’s side first. Clan Chattan was as Jacobite as a Highland confederation gets. Lady Anne Mackintosh raised the regiment for Prince Charles herself while her own husband held a commission in the government army, and Gillies MacBean died in that regiment in the breach. The MacBeans had already paid for the same politics thirty years earlier, when men of the name were transported to the plantations of Virginia and the Carolinas after the rising of 1715. And Clan Donald was the backbone of every Jacobite army that ever took a field. At Culloden the MacDonald regiments held the left wing and were sour about it all day, because they reckoned the right was theirs by right since Bannockburn.

So on my mother’s side: yes. Out on the moor, in the front rank, on the losing side.

Now take my father’s. The Galloway Covenanters were the most implacably anti-Stuart people in Scotland. John McHaffie was taken in 1684 and banished across the Atlantic for refusing to accept that a Stuart could stand at the head of his church. Men were shot in the heather of that country for the same refusal. His descendants were Presbyterian Whigs, and when the Stuarts came back for the throne in 1715 and again in 1745 the Mahaffeys wanted no part of it, because the Stuarts were the family that had hunted their grandfather through the moss.

By 1745 our McHaffies were already in Virginia and nobody in the line fired a shot either way. But their politics had been set sixty years before, and they were the exact opposite of my mother’s people’s.

There is a particular sting in the Campbells.

Clan Donald and Campbell is the great feud of the Highlands. The Lordship of the Isles fell in 1493 and Argyll took the spoils, and Kintyre itself passed out of MacDonald hands to the Campbells in 1607. Now look again at where Margaret Templeton was born. Campbeltown was Ceann Loch Cille Chiarain until the Campbells renamed it for themselves. She was born there in 1698, in a Campbell plantation town raised on ground taken from the clan her son would marry into sixty-two years later in North Carolina.

And yet on my father’s side the Campbells were allies. The ninth Earl of Argyll was executed in 1685 for rising against James VII in the Covenanting cause. Same king, same year, same politics as John McHaffie of Gargerie.

One last thing, because it is the most commonly got wrong. Jacobite did not mean Catholic. Most Jacobites were Episcopalian, which is what the eastern Highlands largely were. Our Galloway line was militantly Presbyterian by definition and stayed that way: Robert and Martha lie in the yard of a Presbyterian church at Mint Hill, and the Argyll Colony’s first act on landing was to write home for a minister who could preach in Gaelic.

So the answer to the question is both. We were on both sides, and we were in earnest both times. Then the whole argument crossed the Atlantic, and generations later my mother married my father, and the two halves of it finally sat down at the same table and had the six of you.

A Dedication for the Six of You

Góða Ólavsøku, my children.

Daniel, Michael: look at the other half of what made that place, and know it is yours by descent every bit as much as the Norse half is yours by birth.

Alexis, standing there for the first time at seventeen without the language, listening to the chain dance and watching the banners: do not feel like an outsider looking through a window.

The very same blood that flows through your veins, the blood of the High Kings of Ireland, the sovereign Lords of Galloway, the unyielding Covenanters of Craigcaffie, the fair-haired highlanders of Clan Chattan, and the magnificent sea-kings of Clan Donald, is the exact same Celtic blood that stepped off the Viking longships to settle the Faroe Islands over a thousand years ago.

The Norse and the Gaels are two halves of the exact same heroic story. Adjust your Faroese dress, lift your chin, and stand with absolute confidence. You are not just celebrating their history today. You are celebrating your own.

Luke, Ashlynn, Liam: your turn will come, and it is the same ground waiting.

Six of you, two countries, one line. Stand together for the picture when you finally get the chance.

The Rule Is Still Running

There is one more thing, and I did not see it until everything else was already written down.

The Scots had a rule for naming children, and it was a system rather than a sentiment. The first son took his father’s father’s name. The second son took his mother’s father’s name. The first daughter took a grandmother’s. You can watch it ticking its way down these pages like a clock.

Robert Bain came ashore in Carolina with nothing and named his first son Daniel, after the Daniel who had married Margaret Templeton and stayed behind in Scotland. His first daughter he named Margaret. His second son he named John MacDonald Bain, which tells us three hundred years later, without a single document, what Martha’s father was called.

On the Mahaffey side the same clock is running. Robert, then William, then Robert, then William, trading places every other generation the whole way back to an Oliver Mahaffey who fought in the Revolution. Nobody kept a chart. Each man simply named his son after his own father, and the pattern looked after itself.

And here is the part I want you to sit with.

My father was John. My eldest son is Daniel. My youngest son is Liam, which is Uilliam, which is William.

I did not do that deliberately. I was not consulting anything. But Daniel carries the name of a man born around 1697 who married a Campbeltown girl and never left Scotland, and Liam carries the name that has been trading places with Robert on my father’s side since before there was a United States. Liam has it in the older language, which I promise you was an accident. Michael and Luke carry the saints’ names our people took up when the Church came, Mìcheal and Lùcas in Gaelic, and if you look back through the Bain family you will find a Matthew and a John waiting for them.

Then there are my two girls, and this is where it stops being funny.

Alexis, I did not name you for anything Scottish. Except Alexander is one of the great Scottish royal names, Alasdair in Gaelic, and a Clan Donald name, and Robert Bain had a son called Alexander born in Carolina in 1777. And Morgan is Clann Mhorgainn, the old name of Clan Mackay out of Strathnaver. I picked two names I liked. Both of them were already ours.

Ashlynn, I did that one on purpose, and I still got more than I bargained for. The name behind yours is Aisling, which means a dream or a vision, and which in Irish poetry is a whole form where the country herself appears to the poet as a woman. And Dallas is the barony of Dallas in Moray, Dalais in Gaelic, from dail and eas: the field by the waterfall. Dail is the field. It is as close to Heath as Gaelic gets, which is why I chose it, and I never told anyone.

What I did not know was the rest. Black’s Surnames of Scotland records Dallas of Cantray and his kinsmen serving with the Mackintosh Regiment at Culloden, and lists the Dallases beside the Macgillivrays, the Macqueens and the MacBeans as families reckoned to Clan Chattan. Cantray is in Nairnshire, next door to Croy, in the middle of MacBean ground. I gave you my own name in Gaelic and it landed on a family that stood in the same regiment my mother’s people died in.

Three hundred years, an ocean, two languages, six of you across two countries, and a family that forgot it was even doing this. The rule went on working anyway. It ran through people who had no idea they were carrying it, and it came out the far end and put those names on the lot of you.

That is what I mean when I say this is yours. Not a story I went and found. A thing that never stopped happening.

Afterword

Sources, and a Few Honest Notes

Everything above that can be checked, I have checked, and the citations are here so you can follow them yourself. Three things I could not confirm, and I would rather you hear that from me than from someone else.

Open questions, for whoever picks this up next

Where the Inverness claim actually comes from.

I traced it. It is a FamilySearch index entry, Scotland Births and Baptisms 1564–1950: Robert Mcbean, 25 April 1738, citing Inverness, Inverness, Scotland, FHL microfilm 990,666 and 990,667. So it is a real record and not somebody guessing from the clan name, which is what I first assumed. But that entry is dated 25 April and ours is given as 11 April, and nothing in it ties the child to Daniel and Margaret. It may be our Robert. It may be another Robert McBean entirely, in a county full of them.

Is Robert even the son of Daniel and Margaret?

This is the hard one, and you should hear it from me. A WikiTree contributor, Joelle Colville-Hanson, posted in May 2019 that there are no real sources placing Robert as the son of Daniel McBain and Margaret Templeton, and that the baptism record attached to him actually belongs to a Robert McBain, son of Daniel McBain and Janet Harvie, born in 1692. If she is right, a 1692 Robert has been mistaken for our 1738 one and the join between those two generations is unproven. A second contributor on the same page says they hold ScotlandsPeople records supporting the line. Write to both of them.

Edinburgh or Campbeltown?

This letter says the 1721 marriage was at Edinburgh. The Geni and WikiTree profiles put it at Campbeltown, Argyll, on the same date, 21 December 1721, with Daniel aged twenty-four. If Campbeltown is right it counts strongly in our favour, because it puts the Bains and the Templetons in one Argyll town rather than meeting somewhere in the Lowlands. The banns entry will say.

The McBean name forms are real, and that part has held up.

He appears across the trees as Daniel McBane, Daniel McBean, Daniel Beane and Daniel Bain, and his sons appear as McBane, McBean, Bean and Bain, sometimes within one family. That is exactly what a Highland family looks like as it passes into English-speaking record keeping. What is still missing is an original register entry rather than a tree entry.

Which Margaret Templeton?

Campbeltown has more than one Margaret Templeton baptised around that time, including one on 16 November 1701. Ours is recorded as 7 August 1698. The parents’ names on the baptism entries are what tell them apart.

1755 or 1775?

Find a Grave gives his arrival in America as 1755. The passenger list source cited on WikiTree gives New York, 1775. Those are twenty years apart, and only one of them fits a man who married in North Carolina in 1760.

Is our Bain line genuinely MacBean of Kinchyle?

Bàn means fair-haired, and Bains arose independently all over Scotland. The Clan Chattan descent is family tradition rather than a proven line. Settling it would take a male-line Bain descendant taking a Y-DNA test, because our own Y-DNA belongs to the Mahaffey side and cannot speak to it at all.

Why the marker reaches your brothers and not you.

The Y chromosome is passed intact from father to son. Daughters receive no Y chromosome from anyone, so a father’s haplogroup cannot be inherited by a daughter and cannot be tested for in her. This says nothing whatsoever about descent: a daughter descends from her paternal ancestors exactly as her brothers do. It is a limitation of one test, not a limitation of a bloodline. If she ever wants the equivalent on her own side, mitochondrial DNA passes mother to child and would trace her mother’s line, which is a different and equally long story that I am not the one to write.

What we can honestly claim about the Faroes, and what we cannot.

We are not claiming that anyone in this family carries a Faroese maternal line. The argument is about ancestral population. The founding mothers of these islands were Gaelic women out of western Scotland and Ireland, and the founding men were largely Norse Gaels out of the same colonies. Our lines, Argyll, the Hebrides, Wigtownshire, are from exactly those places and exactly that people. The kinship is real and it is collateral: we come from the same population that settled these islands, not down a single unbroken thread from it.

The genetic asymmetry is real, and the Faroes are the extreme case.

Jørgensen et al., Human Genetics 115:19–28 (2004), on the Y chromosome, and Als et al., European Journal of Human Genetics 14:497–504 (2006), on mitochondrial DNA. The second paper concludes that of all the admixed populations of the North Atlantic, the Faroe Islands show the highest asymmetry between Scandinavian and British Isles ancestry in their male and female settlers. Commonly cited figures run near 85 to 90 percent Scandinavian on the paternal side and near 85 percent British Isles on the maternal side; exact numbers shift with method, so the “roughly 80 percent” in this letter is the conservative version.

Somebody was here before the Norse, and it does not explain the DNA.

Church et al., Quaternary Science Reviews 77:228–232 (2013). Charred barley in burnt peat beneath a Viking longhouse floor at Á Sondum, Sandoy, dated 300 to 500 years before the Norse. Church himself cautions that this proves early people, not specifically Irish monks. Either way the papar were anchorites and left no descendants, so they are not the source of the Gaelic maternal ancestry in the modern population. That arrived with the settlers.

The settlers were Norse Gaels, and Grímur Kamban is the evidence.

Færeyinga saga names him as the first settler. Grímr is Old Norse; kamban is read as Gaelic, from camb, “crooked,” giving cambán (compare Irish camán, Gaelic caman, Manx camane). A Norse name with a Gaelic byname points to the Norse–Gael settlements of Ireland, the Hebrides or Man rather than to Norway. Faroese tradition places his farm at Funningur on Eysturoy. The later wave of about 885–890 is described as coming mostly from western Norway but with many from Ireland and Scotland. Iceland shows the same asymmetric pattern for the same reason, and Landnámabók names Hebridean and Irish settlers throughout.

Dicuil, and the papar place-names.

Dicuil, De mensura Orbis terrae, written around 825. Identification of his northern islands with the Faroes was debated for a long time and is now widely favored. The Faroese place-names Paparøkur near Vestmanna and Papurshílsur near Saksun preserve the Norse word for those earlier Christian settlers.

Craigcaffie.

Granted by Robert the Bruce as Kellechaffe to John, son of Neil, Earl of Carrick, from whom the Neilsons claimed descent. Note that several popular websites garble this into “Bruce’s illegitimate son Neil.” That is wrong. Niall, Earl of Carrick (d. 1256) was Bruce’s grandfather, and the version in this letter is the correct one. A useful corroboration for our line: Gilbert Neilson of Craigcaffie is recorded as a Covenanter.

First correction: the tower may not be L-plan.

I have described Craigcaffie as an L-plan tower house. The architectural surveys, MacGibbon and Ross in 1889 and RCAHMS in 1912, both describe it as a simple rectangular tower, 30 ft 9 in by 19 ft 9 in. I would check the Historic Environment Scotland listing before repeating the L-plan description.

What the clan genealogy can and cannot date.

The Clann Somhairle descent is documented and gives a usable chronology: Somerled (d. 1164), his son Raghnall (d. after 1192), Raghnall’s sons Domhnall (eponym of Clan Donald) and Ruaidhrí (d. 1247?), and Ruaidhrí’s son Dubhghall (d. 1268), who sent the 1259 gallowglass. An earlier draft of this letter called Dubhghall a grandson of Somerled; he was a great-grandson, and the correction is worth having because the generation count is the whole argument. The Chronicon Manniæ et Insularum names Somerled’s four sons by the daughter of Olaf, King of Man, which is where the Norse royal marriage is recorded.

Robert and Martha, in the record.

Find a Grave memorial 57986584: Robert Bain, born 1738 in Scotland, died 20 April 1801, buried Philadelphia Presbyterian Church Cemetery, Mint Hill, Mecklenburg County, North Carolina; son of Daniel Bain; arrived in America at New York in 1755; married Martha McDonald about 1758 to 1761. Martha has her own stone and her own memorial, 57986626: born 1744 in Scotland, died 1805 in Cabarrus County aged sixty or sixty-one, buried beside him at Philadelphia Presbyterian. Two things there differ from the family version. Her death year is 1805 and not 1801, so she outlived Robert by four years and they did not die together; the 1801 that circulates is his year copied onto her. And WikiTree records Robert’s birth as 11 April 1738 in Inverness-shire, not Stirlingshire. If Inverness-shire is right it is worth a great deal, because that is MacBean country and it would put the family in Clan Chattan’s own ground at birth. I have left Stirlingshire standing in the text because that is what we have always had, but this one is worth chasing.

A correction I owe the Bain side.

An earlier draft of this letter described the MacBeans as followers of Clan Chattan rather than a clan of it. That was wrong. Clan MacBean is a constituent clan of the Chattan confederation with its own chiefly line, the MacBeans of Kinchyle in the parish of Dores, Inverness-shire, and a chief recognised by the Lord Lyon today. Kinchyle signs the Bond of Union (1609), the Bond of Maintenance (1664) and the Letter of Authority to Mackintosh (1756) as a principal. The clan’s own motto is Touch not a catt bot a targe; the “bot a glove” form usually quoted is the Mackintosh variant. What remains genuinely uncertain is only the pre-Chattan origin, where the Lochaber-with-Eva tradition and the Moray/MacBeth tradition compete, and the earliest firm record of the name is the mid fourteenth-century Kinrara manuscript.

The Gallowglass dates, now corrected.

An earlier draft of this letter put the Gallowglass at 800 to 1100 AD. That was the Norse–Gaelic fusion that produced them, not the men themselves. The first recorded gallowglass service is 1259, in the Annals of Connacht and the Annals of Loch Cé: 160 warriors sent to Aedh Ó Conchobair of Connacht as his bride’s dowry from Dubhghall mac Ruaidhrí of the Hebrides, a grandson of Somerled. Recruitment collapsed after Kinsale in 1601, though Highland mercenaries kept coming until the 1640s.

Third correction, and the one I care most about: Niall.

The link between the R1b-M222 marker and Niall of the Nine Hostages comes from a 2006 study and became enormously popular in genealogy. It has been substantially questioned since. The marker is genuinely and densely concentrated in northwest Ireland and western Scotland, which is real and is ours. The attribution to one named fifth-century king is not established, and Niall’s own historicity is debated. Carry the haplogroup with confidence. Hold the king lightly.

The lands really did go to Bruce’s maternal uncle.

Marjorie, Countess of Carrick, was the daughter and heiress of Niall, Earl of Carrick, so John son of Neil was her brother and the King’s uncle. Bruce inherited the Gaelic earldom of Carrick from his mother while his father’s line, the de Brus, came from Normandy. He is generally held to have been a native Gaelic speaker. The Irish campaign of 1315–18, Edward Bruce’s High Kingship, and the Remonstrance of the Irish Princes to Pope John XXII are the documented link to the Uí Néill: Domnall Ua Néill of Tír Eoghain ceded his claim and signed the letter. A blood descent from Niall of the Nine Hostages to the earls of Carrick has never been demonstrated; the Carrick line runs back to Fergus of Galloway.

On Culloden.

The Act of Proscription of 1746 disarmed the clans and banned Highland dress, and it was savage. It did not formally ban the Gaelic language. The suppression of Gaelic ran mostly through schooling and church policy over a longer period.

The McHaffie name has more than one possible root.

This letter takes it from the estate name, Kellechaffe to Craigcaffie to McIlhaffie to McHaffie. Two other derivations are argued: Mac Gille Chathbhaidh, son of the servant of Saint Cathbad, and a Galloway form of MacDhubhshìth, the MacFies or MacDuffies of Colonsay. All three routes still land the family in Galloway.

John McHaffie of Gargerie.

Registers of the Privy Council of Scotland, October 1684, banishment to the American plantations. Gargerie sits in Kirkcowan parish, near Luce Bay. This is your grandfather’s own research and I have not independently verified the entry; the register volumes are the place to confirm it.
